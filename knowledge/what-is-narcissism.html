What is narcissism?

Narcissism is not simply vanity, confidence or selfishness. Psychology describes a complex range of traits involving the self, status, entitlement, admiration, interpersonal functioning and regulation of self esteem.

Evidence led · Deep reference guide · Sources included
Narcissism exists across dimensions
QUICK ANSWER

Narcissism refers to characteristics involving self importance, status, admiration, entitlement and how a person regulates their sense of self in relation to other people.

Narcissistic traits exist to different degrees in the general population. Having some narcissistic traits does not mean somebody has Narcissistic Personality Disorder.

IN DEPTH

Understanding narcissism properly

The word narcissism is now used so casually that it can mean almost anything: arrogance, selfishness, infidelity, confidence, lying, posting selfies, ending a relationship or simply behaving badly.

That is not how psychological science uses the concept.

Narcissism is better understood dimensionally. People vary in characteristics such as self focus, entitlement, desire for admiration, status seeking, grandiosity, sensitivity to evaluation and interpersonal antagonism.

Some degree of self confidence, ambition and concern with how we are perceived by others is part of ordinary human personality. Narcissistic characteristics become more concerning when they are rigid, extreme, persistent or associated with significant interpersonal or psychological problems.

The narcissism spectrum

EVERYDAY TRAITS Ordinary variation

Confidence, ambition, wanting recognition and occasional self centred behaviour can occur without personality pathology.

ELEVATED TRAITS More pronounced patterns

Entitlement, status seeking, admiration needs or interpersonal antagonism may become more prominent.

PATHOLOGICAL Impaired functioning

Grandiosity and vulnerability can become increasingly tied to unstable self esteem and relationship difficulties.

CLINICAL NPD

Narcissistic Personality Disorder is a clinical diagnosis requiring a pervasive pattern and appropriate professional assessment.

Narcissistic traits are not the same as NPD

This is one of the most important distinctions on this page.

A person can be entitled, boastful, validation seeking, arrogant or self centred without meeting diagnostic criteria for Narcissistic Personality Disorder.

NPD concerns a persistent and clinically significant personality pattern involving grandiosity, need for admiration and impaired interpersonal functioning, including difficulties involving empathy. Clinical diagnosis looks at enduring patterns across contexts rather than isolated incidents.

Traits ≠ disorder

You cannot diagnose NPD because somebody lied, cheated, behaved selfishly, became angry, sought attention, used social media heavily or treated one person badly.

Grandiose and vulnerable narcissism

Modern narcissism research frequently distinguishes between grandiose and vulnerable expressions. These are important research dimensions, not two completely separate species of person.

GRANDIOSE

Grandiose narcissism

May be associated with:

  • assertive self enhancement
  • feelings of superiority
  • entitlement
  • status seeking
  • dominance
  • admiration seeking
  • social boldness
  • antagonistic behaviour at higher levels
VULNERABLE

Vulnerable narcissism

May be associated with:

  • hypersensitivity to evaluation
  • shame
  • insecurity
  • defensiveness
  • withdrawal
  • negative emotionality
  • resentment
  • fluctuating self esteem

Research also suggests that grandiose and vulnerable states can fluctuate within individuals. Someone should therefore not be forced into a simplistic “one type only” category.

Are there different “types” of narcissist?

This is where internet psychology becomes particularly confusing. Some terms describe legitimate research dimensions. Others are informal labels rather than recognised diagnostic categories.

TERM WHAT IT ACTUALLY MEANS
Grandiose narcissism

A well established research dimension involving grandiosity, assertive self enhancement, entitlement and status related features.

Vulnerable narcissism

A well established research dimension involving insecurity, hypersensitivity, shame and negative emotionality alongside narcissistic characteristics.

Overt / covert

Descriptive language sometimes used when discussing more visible versus less outwardly obvious narcissistic presentation. These are not separate DSM diagnoses.

Communal narcissism

A research construct describing narcissistic self enhancement in communal domains — for example seeing oneself as exceptionally helpful, caring or morally valuable.

Admiration / rivalry

Research models distinguish self promoting, admiration seeking strategies from antagonistic or rivalrous strategies.

Malignant narcissism

A clinical and theoretical term with a long history, but not a separate DSM-5-TR diagnosis.

Cerebral / somatic narcissist

Common internet terminology. These are not recognised DSM narcissism subtypes.

Possible narcissistic red flags

Red flags are clues that may justify paying closer attention to a behavioural pattern. They are not diagnostic criteria and should never be treated as proof that somebody “is a narcissist”.

01

Persistent entitlement

Repeated expectation of special treatment or exceptions that would not be granted to other people.

02

Excessive admiration needs

A strong or recurring reliance on praise, recognition or validation to maintain a desired self image.

03

Superiority

Persistent presentation of the self as unusually important, exceptional or above ordinary rules.

04

Interpersonal exploitation

Repeatedly treating relationships primarily as ways of obtaining status, validation, advantage or personal gain.

05

Evaluation sensitivity

Strong reactions to criticism, humiliation, defeat or perceived loss of status can occur, particularly in vulnerable states.

06

Status preoccupation

Persistent concern with rank, prestige, recognition, superiority or how one compares with other people.

07

Limited mutuality

Relationships may become disproportionately organised around one person's needs, self esteem or perspective.

08

Antagonism

Rivalry, devaluation, arrogance or hostility may become more prominent in some narcissistic presentations.

A red flag is not a diagnosis

Healthy people sometimes seek reassurance, react defensively, boast, become jealous or behave selfishly. Psychology is concerned with the wider pattern, severity, rigidity, context and consequences.

Things that do not automatically mean narcissism

  • Being confident.
  • Being successful or ambitious.
  • Posting selfies.
  • Wanting praise occasionally.
  • Setting boundaries.
  • Ending a relationship.
  • Disagreeing with somebody.
  • Making a selfish decision.
  • Being angry.
  • Lying on one occasion.
  • Having trauma.
  • Being emotionally distant.
  • Having another mental health condition.

Do narcissistic people have empathy?

“Narcissists have no empathy” is an oversimplification.

Empathy is not one single ability. Researchers distinguish processes such as understanding another person's perspective and sharing or responding emotionally to another person's experience.

Research on pathological narcissism and NPD supports the idea of compromised or inconsistent empathic functioning rather than assuming an absolute inability to experience empathy.

Motivation, emotional regulation, self focus and whether information is perceived as personally relevant may influence empathic responding.

MYTH CHECK

“No empathy” is too simplistic

Empathy difficulties associated with pathological narcissism are more complex than an on/off switch. A person showing empathy in one situation does not automatically rule out narcissistic pathology, and showing poor empathy in another situation does not establish it.

Narcissism and self esteem

Narcissism should not simply be understood as “having extremely high self esteem”.

Pathological narcissistic functioning can involve difficulties maintaining a stable and realistic sense of self worth. Grandiose self enhancement and vulnerable states can both become involved in regulating self esteem.

Approval, admiration, achievement, status and comparison with other people may become particularly important sources of self evaluation.

Why criticism can matter

Criticism can threaten the self image of anyone. In narcissistic vulnerability, however, evaluation, rejection, humiliation or loss of status may be experienced especially intensely.

Possible responses can include shame, defensiveness, withdrawal, anger or attempts to restore a more favourable sense of self.

None of these responses alone identifies narcissism.

Narcissism in relationships

Narcissistic traits can influence relationships when self esteem regulation, admiration, entitlement or status begin to interfere with reciprocity and genuine mutuality.

Difficulties may involve making space for another person's needs, tolerating criticism, negotiating equality, responding to another perspective or maintaining stable views of self and others during conflict.

However, relationship problems do not prove NPD. Many completely different psychological and situational processes can cause unhealthy relationships.

Narcissism, manipulation and abuse are not synonyms

Another major internet error is treating narcissism, manipulation and abuse as if they were interchangeable words.

They are not.

People can behave abusively without having NPD. A person can have narcissistic traits without being abusive. A person with a diagnosed personality disorder should not automatically be assumed to be an abuser.

Harmful behaviour should be evaluated on its own evidence rather than requiring a psychiatric label to make it serious.

Is gaslighting proof of narcissism?

No. Gaslighting describes a form of psychological manipulation involving the undermining of another person's confidence in their perception, memory or reality.

Gaslighting-like behaviour does not diagnose NPD.

Is DARVO proof of narcissism?

No. DARVO describes denial, attack and reversal of victim and offender roles following confrontation about alleged wrongdoing.

It is a behavioural concept rather than a diagnostic test for NPD.

What causes narcissism?

There is no scientifically established single cause that explains narcissistic personality development in every individual.

Personality develops through complex interactions between biological disposition, temperament, developmental experiences and environmental influences.

Genetic research indicates that narcissistic characteristics and personality disorder liability can contain heritable components, while environmental experiences also contribute.

This means simplistic statements such as “narcissists are created by bad parenting” or “all narcissism comes from childhood trauma” go beyond what the evidence can establish.

What is Narcissistic Personality Disorder?

Narcissistic Personality Disorder is a recognised personality disorder in the DSM-5-TR.

Clinical diagnosis involves a pervasive pattern rather than a collection of isolated “red flags”. Assessment considers personality functioning, interpersonal behaviour, grandiosity, admiration needs, empathy and other relevant features across time and situations.

Personality pathology must also be distinguished from temporary responses, developmental stages, other mental health conditions, substance effects and situational behaviour.

How is narcissism measured in research?

Researchers use several different instruments because narcissism is not a single simple dimension.

The Narcissistic Personality Inventory has historically been widely used in research concerning grandiose narcissistic traits, while the Pathological Narcissism Inventory was developed to assess both grandiose and vulnerable dimensions of pathological narcissism.

Research questionnaires are not the same thing as a clinical diagnosis. A score on an online narcissism test should not be used to diagnose yourself, a partner, an ex partner, a parent or anybody else.

Can narcissistic traits change?

Personality has relative stability, but it is not completely frozen. Individuals and their behaviour can change over time.

Clinical literature also rejects the simplistic idea that everybody with NPD is permanently incapable of improvement. Treatment can be challenging and change may be gradual, but improvement is possible.

Treatment needs to be individualised and may focus on self esteem regulation, relationships, emotional functioning, reflective ability and realistic goals.

Narcissism myths vs evidence

MYTH “Every narcissist is an abuser.”

Abuse and narcissism are different constructs. Harmful behaviour should be assessed directly.

MYTH “Narcissists have zero empathy.”

Research supports more variable and compromised empathic functioning rather than a universal absence of all empathy.

MYTH “Confidence means narcissism.”

Confidence alone is not evidence of pathological narcissism.

MYTH “Covert narcissist is a diagnosis.”

It is not a separate DSM-5-TR diagnosis.

MYTH “A narcissist can never change.”

Personality pathology can be difficult to treat, but permanent impossibility of change is not supported as a universal rule.

MYTH “You can diagnose one from a checklist.”

Clinical diagnosis requires substantially more evidence and assessment than an internet checklist.

What if someone's behaviour is harming you?

You do not need to prove that somebody is a narcissist before taking harmful behaviour seriously.

  • Describe the behaviour rather than relying only on labels.
  • Pay attention to repeated patterns and their impact.
  • Maintain appropriate personal boundaries.
  • Keep perspective by speaking to trusted independent people.
  • Seek professional support when your wellbeing is affected.
  • Prioritise safety where coercion, violence or serious abuse is present.

A psychiatric label should never be the price of admission for recognising that a relationship is unhealthy.

Frequently asked questions

Is everyone a little narcissistic?

Narcissistic characteristics exist dimensionally, so people can show different degrees of traits associated with narcissism. That does not mean everyone has pathological narcissism or NPD.

Does selfish behaviour mean somebody is narcissistic?

No. Selfish behaviour can occur for many reasons. Narcissism concerns broader patterns and dimensions of personality.

Are grandiose and vulnerable narcissism real?

Yes. They are widely studied dimensions in narcissism research, although they should not be mistaken for two separate DSM diagnoses.

Is “covert narcissist” an official diagnosis?

No. Vulnerable narcissism is a legitimate research construct, but “covert narcissist” is not a separate DSM-5-TR diagnosis.

Do narcissistic people know they are narcissistic?

Self awareness varies between individuals. It cannot be assumed that everyone with elevated narcissistic traits has either complete insight or complete lack of insight.

Can somebody have narcissistic traits without NPD?

Yes. This is a fundamental distinction. Traits exist across the population and do not automatically constitute a personality disorder.

Can a person with narcissistic traits love somebody?

Psychological science does not support reducing every person with narcissistic traits or NPD to an inability to experience attachment or care. Interpersonal functioning can be impaired, but individuals vary considerably.

Does trauma cause narcissism?

Development is complex. No single developmental experience explains narcissism in every person, and genetic, environmental and psychological factors may all contribute.

Can women be narcissistic?

Yes. Narcissistic traits and NPD are not restricted to one sex or gender.

Does gaslighting mean somebody has NPD?

No. A manipulation tactic cannot independently diagnose a personality disorder.

Should I call someone a narcissist?

In most real world conflicts it is more precise to describe the behaviour that concerns you rather than presenting an unverified clinical label as fact.
