What are Cluster B personality disorders?

Cluster B is a traditional diagnostic grouping containing antisocial, borderline, histrionic and narcissistic personality disorders. This guide explains what the term means, where it came from, how the four diagnoses differ, why they overlap and why individual behaviour can never establish a diagnosis.

Four distinct diagnoses with areas of possible overlap

Quick answer

What does Cluster B mean?

Cluster B is the name traditionally given to four personality disorder diagnoses in the American Psychiatric Association's categorical diagnostic system: antisocial personality disorder, borderline personality disorder, histrionic personality disorder and narcissistic personality disorder.

They were grouped together because their diagnostic descriptions can involve difficulties with emotional regulation, impulse control, identity, relationships or attention seeking. The historic description “dramatic, emotional or erratic” is a broad organising label, not a description of every person who receives one of these diagnoses.

Essential distinction: Cluster B is not a synonym for abusive, dangerous, selfish or manipulative. A person may behave harmfully without having a personality disorder, and somebody with a diagnosed personality disorder should not automatically be assumed to be abusive.

Diagnostic context

Cluster B is one model, not the whole of personality science

The cluster model divides ten named personality disorders into Clusters A, B and C. It remains widely discussed online and is retained in the DSM tradition. However, personality difficulties do not always fit neatly into one box. People can meet criteria across diagnoses, experience traits at different levels and change considerably over time.

ICD-11 takes a substantially different approach. It first considers whether a personality disorder is present, then describes its severity and prominent trait domains. A borderline pattern qualifier can also be recorded. This dimensional approach reflects the reality that personality pathology often exists on continua rather than as four completely separate kinds of person.

DSM-style categories and ICD-11 dimensions

The categorical question asks which named disorder best describes the presentation. The dimensional question asks how severe the impairment is and which traits are most prominent. Clinicians may need to understand both systems, particularly when reading international research.

The four diagnoses

What disorders are included in Cluster B?

Antisocial personality disorder

The diagnostic picture centres on a persistent pattern of disregarding or violating other people's rights, alongside features such as deceit, irresponsibility, aggression, impulsivity or lack of remorse. Diagnosis requires a detailed developmental and behavioural history. It is not established simply because somebody broke a rule, lied or committed an offence.

Borderline personality disorder

The presentation can include marked instability in emotions, relationships, self-image and behaviour. Some people experience intense fear of abandonment, rapidly changing emotional states, chronic emptiness, self-harm, suicidal behaviour, dissociation or difficulty maintaining a stable sense of self. Experiences vary greatly between individuals.

Histrionic personality disorder

The traditional diagnosis describes a pervasive pattern involving strong attention seeking and emotionally expressive interpersonal behaviour. The label is controversial because culture, gender expectations and clinician bias can affect how expressiveness is judged. Being outgoing, theatrical, flirtatious or emotional does not itself demonstrate a disorder.

Narcissistic personality disorder

The diagnostic picture involves persistent problems relating to grandiosity, the need for admiration and empathy, together with significant impairment. Presentations can include overt confidence, defensiveness, vulnerability, shame, entitlement or unstable self-esteem. Narcissistic traits alone are not the same as NPD.

These descriptions summarise areas of clinical emphasis rather than defining every diagnosed person. Presentations are individual, overlap is common and contemporary dimensional systems may describe severity and traits instead of relying only on named categories.

Overlap

What can the four disorders have in common?

The four diagnoses are not interchangeable, but their criteria and associated difficulties can overlap. Areas sometimes shared across presentations include:

  • unstable or conflict-filled relationships;
  • difficulty regulating intense emotional states;
  • impulsive decisions or behaviour;
  • sensitivity to rejection, criticism, status or abandonment;
  • an unstable, threatened or highly defended sense of self;
  • difficulty understanding or responding to another person's perspective;
  • attempts to manage distress through attention, control, avoidance or reassurance;
  • coexisting depression, anxiety, trauma symptoms or substance misuse.

These are broad psychological difficulties rather than diagnostic proof. Many occur in trauma-related conditions, mood disorders, neurodevelopmental conditions, substance-related problems and ordinary reactions to severe stress.

Clinical assessment

Traits are not the same as a personality disorder

Everyone can act impulsively, seek reassurance, become defensive, crave attention or struggle with empathy under pressure. A personality disorder concerns enduring patterns that are inflexible, occur across contexts and cause meaningful distress, impairment or harm.

Clinicians consider duration, severity, development, context, functioning, culture, physical health, substance use and alternative explanations. They also assess coexisting conditions. A checklist assembled from arguments, social media posts or one person's description cannot perform that work.

Do not diagnose from a relationship conflict. Labelling an ex-partner, parent, colleague or public figure “Cluster B” may feel explanatory, but it can replace evidence with certainty and can increase stigma towards people who have genuine mental health needs.

Development

What causes Cluster B personality disorders?

There is no single cause. Current models examine interacting biological, developmental, psychological and social influences. Genetic differences in temperament may affect emotional sensitivity, impulsivity or reward seeking. Developmental adversity, inconsistent caregiving, neglect, abuse, unstable environments and disrupted attachment may increase vulnerability for some people, but none guarantees a later diagnosis.

Protective relationships, social support, learning, culture and access to treatment also shape development. Two people exposed to similar adversity may have very different outcomes. Likewise, a diagnosis does not prove that a particular childhood event occurred.

Differential understanding

Why do Cluster B diagnoses overlap?

Diagnostic overlap can occur because categories share criteria, because a person genuinely experiences several patterns or because another condition better explains part of the presentation. Trauma, ADHD, autism, bipolar disorder, depression, anxiety, substance use and dissociation can sometimes resemble individual features associated with personality disorder.

This does not mean those conditions are the same. It means assessment must examine the pattern over time rather than matching isolated behaviours to a label.

Relationships

Cluster B and difficult relationship behaviour

Some people with Cluster B diagnoses experience serious relational difficulties. These might involve fear-driven reassurance seeking, withdrawal, emotional escalation, unstable idealisation and disappointment, boundary problems, impulsive behaviour or attempts to control uncertainty.

Nevertheless, diagnosis does not determine character. Abuse must be assessed through behaviour, impact, pattern and safety—not inferred from a psychiatric label. People without personality disorders can engage in coercive control, deception or violence, while many diagnosed people do not.

Judge behaviour directly

Useful questions include: Is there intimidation? Are boundaries repeatedly ignored? Is someone being isolated, threatened, monitored or financially controlled? Can responsibility be discussed safely? These questions are more protective than attempting to diagnose the person responsible.

Professional diagnosis

How are personality disorders assessed?

NICE recommends structured clinical assessment by appropriately trained mental health professionals. Assessment may include validated interviews, developmental history, current functioning, risk, physical and mental health, substance use, relationships, culture and information gathered over time.

A responsible assessment also considers diagnostic bias. Gender, ethnicity, socioeconomic circumstances, trauma histories and cultural expectations can influence how behaviour is interpreted and which diagnosis is considered.

Treatment and recovery

Can Cluster B personality disorders be treated?

Yes. Personality patterns are not necessarily fixed for life. Outcomes differ, but meaningful improvement is possible. Treatment is individualised and often focuses on emotional regulation, relationships, impulsivity, mentalising, coping, identity, trauma where relevant and practical functioning.

Psychological approaches may include dialectical behaviour therapy, mentalisation-based treatment, schema therapy, cognitive behavioural approaches and other structured therapies. The suitability of any treatment depends on the person's needs, diagnosis, risks, preferences and available services.

Medication may be used for coexisting conditions or carefully defined short-term needs, but medication is not a universal treatment for a personality disorder itself. Treatment planning belongs with qualified healthcare professionals.

Stigma and misinformation

Common myths about Cluster B

“Everyone with Cluster B is abusive.”

False. Abuse is behaviour, not a diagnosis. Automatically linking a mental health diagnosis with abuse is inaccurate and stigmatising.

“Cluster B means narcissist.”

False. Narcissistic personality disorder is only one of four diagnoses in the traditional cluster, and narcissistic traits can occur without NPD.

“People with personality disorders never change.”

False. Symptoms and functioning can improve, sometimes substantially, particularly with suitable support, treatment and sustained engagement.

“A difficult childhood proves the diagnosis.”

False. Adversity may be one vulnerability factor, but it neither proves nor inevitably causes a personality disorder.

“A relationship checklist can identify Cluster B.”

False. Online material can support education, but diagnosis requires a structured professional assessment and consideration of alternatives.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Is psychopathy a Cluster B diagnosis?

Psychopathy is not itself one of the four DSM Cluster B personality disorder diagnoses. It is a research and forensic construct with some overlap with antisocial personality disorder, but the terms are not interchangeable.

Can somebody have more than one Cluster B diagnosis?

Diagnostic criteria can overlap, and some people meet criteria for more than one personality disorder. This overlap is one reason dimensional models have become increasingly important.

Is borderline personality disorder the same as bipolar disorder?

No. They are distinct diagnoses. Both may involve changes in mood, but the pattern, duration, triggers, associated symptoms and clinical assessment differ.

Can teenagers be diagnosed with a personality disorder?

Assessment in adolescence requires particular developmental care. Clinicians must distinguish persistent patterns from normal development, temporary crises, trauma responses and other conditions.

Should I tell somebody that I think they are Cluster B?

Usually it is more constructive to describe specific behaviour, its impact and the boundary or support required. Diagnosis should be left to qualified professionals working directly with the person.
