Fight, flight, freeze and fawn

Fight, flight, freeze and fawn are four interconnected survival responses to trauma, each with distinct characteristics and consequences. Understanding these responses is crucial for developing effective coping strategies and promoting emotional well-being.

What is it?

Understanding the Fight, Flight, Freeze and Fawn Responses

The fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses are a set of automatic physiological and psychological reactions that occur in response to perceived threats or stressors. These responses are often referred to as the body's 'stress response' or 'fight or flight response', although they encompass more than just fighting or fleeing. The fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses are fundamental to human survival and are triggered by the release of hormones such as adrenaline and cortisol.

When we perceive a threat, our body's 'threat detection system' is activated, sending a distress signal to the brain. This signal triggers the release of stress hormones, which prepare the body for action. The brain then responds by sending signals to the muscles, heart, and other organs to prepare for the body's chosen response. In a typical fight or flight response, the body prepares to either fight the threat or flee from it. However, in situations where the threat is perceived as overwhelming or uncontrollable, other responses may be triggered, including freezing or fawning.

Freezing is a response where the individual becomes immobile and unable to move or respond. This response is often seen in situations where the individual feels trapped or unable to escape. Fawning, on the other hand, is a response where the individual attempts to appease the threat by being overly submissive or compliant. This response is often seen in situations where the individual feels powerless or vulnerable.

It's essential to understand that these responses are not just simple reactions to stress; they are complex physiological and psychological processes that involve multiple systems and structures in the body. The fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses are highly adaptive and have evolved to help humans survive in a wide range of situations. However, in the context of trauma, these responses can become maladaptive and lead to long-term negative consequences for mental and physical health.

The fight response is often characterised by increased muscle tension, increased heart rate, and a sense of readiness to engage with the threat. This response is typically associated with a sense of confidence and assertiveness. In contrast, the flight response is often characterised by a desire to escape or flee from the threat. This response is typically associated with a sense of fear and anxiety.

The freeze response is often characterised by a sense of immobility and disconnection from the body. This response is typically associated with a sense of fear and helplessness. The fawn response is often characterised by a sense of submission and compliance. This response is typically associated with a sense of shame and self-blame.

It's worth noting that these responses are not mutually exclusive, and individuals may exhibit a combination of responses in response to a single threat. For example, an individual may initially freeze in response to a threat, but then switch to a fight response if they feel a sense of confidence and assertiveness. Alternatively, an individual may exhibit a fawn response in response to a threat, but then switch to a flight response if they feel a sense of fear and anxiety.

Understanding the fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses is essential for developing effective strategies for managing trauma and promoting recovery. By learning to recognise and regulate these responses, individuals can develop greater control over their emotions and behaviours, and improve their overall well-being.

The fight response is often associated with increased levels of testosterone and other androgens. This response is typically associated with a sense of confidence and assertiveness. However, in the context of trauma, excessive flight response can lead to avoidance and escapism, which can exacerbate symptoms of anxiety and depression.

This response is typically associated with a sense of disconnection and dissociation from the body. However, in the context of trauma, excessive freeze response can lead to feelings of numbness and detachment from others.

The Origins of Fight, Flight, Freeze and Fawn Responses

The origins of fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses can be understood by examining the physiological and psychological processes that govern human behaviour in the face of perceived threats. The fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses are often referred to as the 'four Fs', and are thought to have evolved as adaptive mechanisms to help individuals respond to danger and increase their chances of survival.

From a physiological perspective, the fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses are mediated by the body's autonomic nervous system (ANS). The ANS is responsible for regulating various bodily functions, including heart rate, blood pressure, and respiration. In response to a perceived threat, the ANS activates the sympathetic nervous system (SNS), which prepares the body for action by increasing heart rate, blood pressure, and respiration. This is often referred to as the 'fight or flight' response.

However, not all individuals respond to threats in the same way. Some people may experience a 'freeze' response, where they become immobile and unable to take action. This response is thought to be an evolutionary adaptation that helps individuals avoid detection by predators. In some cases, individuals may even experience a 'fawn' response, where they become overly submissive and accommodating in an attempt to appease the perceived threat. This response is thought to be an evolutionary adaptation that helps individuals avoid conflict and maintain social bonds.

The fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses are not unique to humans, and can be observed in many other animals. In fact, the concept of the 'four Fs' was first proposed by ethologist ethologist, who observed similar responses in animals such as deer and rabbits. However, the human experience of the fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses is complex and influenced by a range of factors, including genetics, environment, and past experiences.

One key factor that influences the fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses is the concept of neuroplasticity. Neuroplasticity refers to the brain's ability to reorganize and adapt in response to new experiences and environments. In the context of trauma, neuroplasticity can play a significant role in shaping the fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses. For example, individuals who have experienced trauma may develop a more sensitive stress response system, which can lead to an exaggerated fight or flight response. Conversely, individuals who have experienced chronic stress may develop a more suppressed stress response system, which can lead to a freeze response.

Another key factor that influences the fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses is the concept of conditioning. Conditioning refers to the process of learning to associate certain stimuli with certain responses. In the context of trauma, conditioning can play a significant role in shaping the fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses. For example, individuals who have experienced trauma may develop a conditioned response to certain stimuli, such as loud noises or certain smells, which can trigger a fight or flight response. Conversely, individuals who have experienced chronic stress may develop a conditioned response to certain stimuli, such as a certain tone of voice, which can trigger a freeze response.

In addition to neuroplasticity and conditioning, other factors can influence the fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses, including genetics, environment, and past experiences. For example, individuals who have a family history of trauma may be more likely to develop a fight or flight response to stress. Conversely, individuals who have experienced chronic stress may be more likely to develop a freeze response. The interplay between these factors can be complex and influenced by a range of individual differences.

Furthermore, the fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses are not mutually exclusive, and individuals may exhibit a combination of responses in response to a perceived threat. For example, an individual may exhibit a fight response in the face of immediate danger, but then switch to a freeze response when they feel overwhelmed or unable to cope. Conversely, an individual may exhibit a fawn response in the face of a perceived threat, but then switch to a fight response when they feel empowered or able to take action.

The understanding of the fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses is essential for developing effective strategies for managing trauma and promoting resilience. By recognising the complex interplay between neuroplasticity, conditioning, genetics, environment, and past experiences, individuals can begin to understand why they respond to stress in certain ways. This understanding can be used to develop personalized strategies for managing trauma and promoting resilience, and can help individuals to develop a more adaptive and effective stress response system.

Ultimately, the fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses are complex and multifaceted, and are influenced by a range of individual differences. This assessment is primarily handled by the amygdala, a small almond-shaped structure located in the temporal lobe. The amygdala processes sensory information and sends signals to other parts of the brain, including the hypothalamus, which initiates the physiological response.

Research suggests that the hypothalamus plays a crucial role in the regulation of the body's stress response, often referred to as the 'fight or flight' response. This response is characterised by the release of stress hormones, such as adrenaline and cortisol, which prepare the body for action. The individual may experience increased heart rate, blood pressure, and respiration, as well as heightened sensory awareness and a sense of alertness.

However, not all individuals respond to threats with the classic fight or flight response. Some may experience a freeze response, characterised by immobility and a sense of being 'stuck'. This response is often seen in situations where the individual feels overwhelmed or powerless, and is associated with a range of negative emotions, including fear, anxiety, and despair.

The freeze response is thought to be an adaptive mechanism, allowing the individual to conserve energy and avoid further danger. However, in some cases, this response can be maladaptive, leading to feelings of helplessness and a lack of control. Research has shown that individuals who experience a freeze response may be more likely to develop anxiety disorders, such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).

Another response to perceived threats is the fawn response, which is characterised by a desire to please and appease others. This response is often seen in individuals who have experienced trauma, particularly in situations where they felt powerless or vulnerable. The fawn response is thought to be an attempt to regain control and safety, by attempting to meet the needs of others.

Research suggests that the fawn response is associated with a range of negative outcomes, including anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem. Individuals who exhibit a fawn response may struggle with setting boundaries, assert themselves, and prioritize their own needs. This can lead to feelings of resentment and burnout, as well as a range of physical symptoms, including headaches and fatigue.

The fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses are not mutually exclusive, and individuals may exhibit a combination of these responses depending on the situation. One of the most well-documented comorbidities of trauma is depression. Research suggests that individuals who have experienced trauma are at a higher risk of developing depression, with some studies indicating that up to 80% of individuals with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) also meet the diagnostic criteria for depression. Depression can manifest in a variety of ways, including feelings of sadness, hopelessness, and loss of interest in activities that were once enjoyable. In individuals who have experienced trauma, depression can be particularly challenging to treat, as it may be deeply intertwined with the trauma itself.

Another common comorbidity of trauma is anxiety. Anxiety disorders, including generalised anxiety disorder, panic disorder, and social anxiety disorder, are all more prevalent among individuals who have experienced trauma. Anxiety can manifest in a variety of ways, including feelings of fear, worry, and apprehension. In individuals who have experienced trauma, anxiety can be particularly debilitating, as it may be triggered by reminders of the traumatic event or by situations that resemble the traumatic experience.

Substance use disorders are also a common comorbidity of trauma. Research suggests that individuals who have experienced trauma are at a higher risk of developing substance use disorders, including alcohol use disorder and opioid use disorder. Substance use can serve as a coping mechanism for individuals who have experienced trauma, helping them to temporarily escape from their emotions and memories. However, substance use can also exacerbate symptoms of trauma, leading to a vicious cycle of substance use and trauma symptoms.

Borderline personality disorder (BPD) is another comorbidity that is commonly seen in individuals who have experienced trauma. BPD is characterised by a pattern of unstable relationships, emotional dysregulation, and impulsivity. Individuals with BPD may be more prone to self-harm and suicidal behaviour, and may have difficulty maintaining stable relationships. Research suggests that individuals with BPD are more likely to have experienced trauma, particularly childhood trauma, and that trauma may play a role in the development of BPD.

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a common comorbidity of trauma, and is characterised by symptoms such as flashbacks, nightmares, and avoidance of reminders of the traumatic event. PTSD can be particularly challenging to treat, as it may be deeply intertwined with the trauma itself. Complex post-traumatic stress disorder (C-PTSD) is a condition that is characterised by symptoms that are similar to PTSD, but are more complex and multifaceted. C-PTSD is often seen in individuals who have experienced prolonged and repeated trauma, such as in cases of childhood abuse or domestic violence. C-PTSD can be particularly challenging to treat, as it may require a more comprehensive and individualized treatment approach.

Other comorbidities that are commonly seen in individuals who have experienced trauma include eating disorders, such as anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa. Eating disorders can be particularly challenging to treat, as they may be deeply intertwined with the trauma itself. Another crucial aspect of managing trauma responses is self-regulation. This involves developing skills to regulate one's physiological and emotional responses to traumatic stimuli. Self-regulation can be achieved through various techniques, including progressive muscle relaxation, visualization, and physical exercise. These techniques can help individuals manage their physical sensations, such as tension and anxiety, and reduce their emotional reactivity to traumatic stimuli.

Grounding techniques are also essential for managing trauma responses. Grounding involves focusing one's attention on the present moment, often through sensory experiences, to help manage dissociation and reduce feelings of disconnection. Debunking Common Misconceptions About Fight, Flight, Freeze and Fawn Responses

Debunking Common Misconceptions About Fight, Flight, Freeze and Fawn Responses

Understanding the fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses is crucial for developing effective strategies to cope with trauma. However, numerous misconceptions surrounding these responses can hinder this process. In this section, we will address some of the most common myths and misconceptions about fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses.

Myth 1: The Fight Response is Always Aggressive

One common misconception is that the fight response is always aggressive and confrontational. While it is true that some individuals may exhibit aggressive behaviour when in a fight response, this is not always the case. The fight response is a primal instinct that allows an individual to defend themselves against perceived threats. It can manifest in various ways, including assertive communication, standing up for oneself, or even simply drawing boundaries. The key aspect of the fight response is not aggression, but rather a desire to protect oneself and maintain a sense of control.

Myth 2: The Flight Response is Always Running Away

Another misconception is that the flight response is always about physically running away from a situation. While physical flight may be a manifestation of this response, it is not the only way it can manifest. The flight response can also involve mental escape, such as daydreaming, fantasizing, or becoming distracted by external stimuli. Additionally, some individuals may exhibit a 'freeze' response, where they become immobile and disengage from their surroundings. This is often a coping mechanism to avoid feeling overwhelmed or vulnerable.

Myth 3: The Freeze Response is a Sign of Weakness

The freeze response is often misunderstood as a sign of weakness or lack of courage. However, this is not the case. The freeze response is a natural coping mechanism that allows an individual to conserve energy and avoid feeling overwhelmed by a traumatic situation. When an individual freezes, their body's 'fight or flight' response is suppressed, and they may appear to be 'going limp' or becoming immobile. This response is not a sign of weakness, but rather a desperate attempt to survive and maintain a sense of control.

Myth 4: The Fawn Response is Always People-Pleasing

The fawn response is often misunderstood as being solely about people-pleasing. While it is true that some individuals may exhibit fawning behaviour to avoid conflict or maintain a sense of control, this is not the only manifestation of this response. The fawn response can also involve a deep-seated need for approval and validation, often stemming from early life experiences or trauma. Individuals who exhibit fawning behaviour may do so because they feel a deep sense of insecurity or inadequacy, and are desperate to be accepted and loved.

Myth 5: Trauma Survivors Always Exhibit One Dominant Response

Another misconception is that trauma survivors always exhibit one dominant response, such as fight or flight. However, this is not the case. Trauma survivors may exhibit a combination of different responses, or their responses may shift depending on the situation. For example, an individual may exhibit a fight response in one situation and a freeze response in another. This is because the brain's response to trauma is complex and multifaceted, and can change depending on the individual's circumstances and experiences.

Myth 6: The Fight, Flight, Freeze and Fawn Responses are Mutually Exclusive

Finally, some individuals may believe that the fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses are mutually exclusive, meaning that an individual can only exhibit one response at a time. However, this is not the case. In reality, these responses can co-occur, or an individual may exhibit a combination of different responses. For example, an individual may exhibit a fight response in one situation, while also experiencing a freeze response in another. This is because the brain's response to trauma is complex and multifaceted, and can change depending on the individual's circumstances and experiences.

By understanding these common misconceptions and myths surrounding the fight, flight, freeze and fawn responses, we can develop more effective strategies for coping with trauma and promoting healing. It is essential to approach each individual with compassion and understanding, recognising that their response to trauma is unique and complex.

Neurological Impact

The Long-Term Effects of Fight, Flight, Freeze and Fawn Responses on Brain Function

The fight, flight, freeze, and fawn responses are complex physiological reactions that help individuals cope with acute threats. While these responses are designed to be temporary and adaptive, chronic activation of these systems can have long-term effects on brain function, leading to changes in the structure and function of various brain regions.

One of the primary ways in which fight, flight, freeze, and fawn responses impact brain function is through the release of stress hormones, such as cortisol and adrenaline. These hormones play a crucial role in the body's 'fight or flight' response, preparing the body to either confront or flee from a threat. However, when these hormones are released in excess, they can have negative effects on the brain, including the suppression of the hippocampus, a region critical for learning and memory.

Research suggests that chronic stress can lead to a decrease in the volume of the hippocampus, as well as a reduction in the number of neurons in this region. This can have significant consequences for an individual's ability to learn and remember new information, making it more difficult to form and maintain healthy relationships, and increasing the risk of developing anxiety and depression.

Another area of the brain that is impacted by chronic stress is the prefrontal cortex, a region responsible for executive function, decision-making, and impulse control. The prefrontal cortex is heavily reliant on the neurotransmitter dopamine, which is released in response to rewarding or pleasurable activities. However, when the body is in a state of chronic stress, the release of dopamine is suppressed, leading to a decrease in motivation and pleasure.

Furthermore, chronic activation of the fight, flight, freeze, and fawn responses can lead to changes in the structure and function of the amygdala, a region critical for the processing of emotions. The amygdala is responsible for detecting threats and triggering the body's stress response, but when it is overactive, it can lead to an exaggerated fear response and increased anxiety.

Additionally, chronic stress can lead to changes in the structure and function of the insula, a region involved in interoception, or the perception of bodily sensations. The insula plays a critical role in regulating the body's response to stress, and when it is impaired, it can lead to a range of symptoms, including anxiety, depression, and chronic pain.

It's also worth noting that the effects of fight, flight, freeze, and fawn responses on brain function can be influenced by individual differences in temperament and personality. For example, individuals with a more anxious or sensitive temperament may be more prone to developing anxiety and depression in response to chronic stress, while individuals with a more resilient temperament may be better equipped to cope with stress and adversity.

Moreover, the effects of fight, flight, freeze, and fawn responses on brain function can also be influenced by early life experiences and trauma. For example, individuals who experience trauma or neglect in early childhood may be more prone to developing anxiety and depression in response to chronic stress, due to changes in the structure and function of the brain that occur as a result of early life adversity.

Finally, it's worth noting that the effects of fight, flight, freeze, and fawn responses on brain function can be reversed through various forms of therapy and treatment, including cognitive-behavioural therapy, mindfulness-based interventions, and trauma-focused therapies. These interventions can help individuals to develop more adaptive coping strategies, reduce symptoms of anxiety and depression, and improve overall brain function.

Research suggests that the brain is highly plastic and capable of reorganizing itself in response to new experiences and learning. This process, known as neuroplasticity, allows individuals to develop new neural connections and pathways, and to compensate for damage or dysfunction in existing brain regions. By engaging in activities that promote neuroplasticity, such as exercise, meditation, and cognitive training, individuals can help to reverse the negative effects of fight, flight, freeze, and fawn responses on brain function and improve overall mental health and wellbeing.

Overall, the fight, flight, freeze, and fawn responses have a profound impact on brain function, leading to changes in the structure and function of various brain regions. However, by understanding the mechanisms underlying these responses, and by engaging in activities that promote neuroplasticity and resilience, individuals can help to mitigate the negative effects of chronic stress and improve overall mental health and wellbeing.

One key concept that is crucial to understanding the effects of fight, flight, freeze, and fawn responses on brain function is the idea of neurotransmitter imbalance. Neurotransmitters are chemicals that transmit signals between neurons, and an imbalance of these chemicals can have significant effects on mood, motivation, and cognitive function. For example, an excess of cortisol and adrenaline can lead to a decrease in the production of neurotransmitters such as serotonin and dopamine, which are involved in mood regulation and motivation.

Another key concept is the idea of neuroinflammation, which refers to the activation of immune cells in the brain in response to stress and adversity. Neuroinflammation can lead to changes in the structure and function of brain regions, and can contribute to the development of anxiety and depression.

Healing and Recovery

The Process of Recovery and Healing from Trauma

The process of recovery and healing from trauma is complex, multifaceted, and deeply personal. It involves a journey of self-discovery, growth, and transformation, as individuals learn to confront, process, and integrate their traumatic experiences into their sense of identity and self.

For many people, the initial response to trauma is to enter a state of hyperarousal, characterised by increased heart rate, blood pressure, and respiration. This can lead to feelings of anxiety, hypervigilance, and an exaggerated startle response. As the body attempts to return to a state of homeostasis, it may also enter a state of dissociation, where the individual disconnects from their emotions, thoughts, and bodily sensations.

However, dissociation can provide a temporary sense of relief, allowing individuals to cope with the overwhelming emotions and sensations associated with trauma. Unfortunately, it can also prevent them from fully processing and integrating their experiences, leading to ongoing symptoms and distress.

One of the key challenges of recovery from trauma is the need to re-establish a sense of safety and trust. This may involve rebuilding relationships with others, developing coping strategies, and learning to regulate one's emotions and bodily responses. It may also require a process of self-compassion, as individuals learn to acknowledge and accept their vulnerabilities and limitations.

Research suggests that the process of recovery from trauma involves a series of distinct stages, each with its own unique challenges and opportunities for growth. These stages may include:

  • Acceptance: This involves acknowledging the reality of one's traumatic experiences and taking responsibility for one's own healing and recovery.
  • Processing: This involves working through the emotions, thoughts, and bodily sensations associated with trauma, and integrating them into one's sense of self.
  • Integration: This involves bringing together the different aspects of one's self, including the traumatic experiences, and developing a new sense of identity and purpose.
  • Renewal: This involves emerging from the recovery process with a renewed sense of hope, resilience, and purpose.

It's worth noting that these stages are not necessarily linear, and individuals may move back and forth between them as they work through their recovery. Additionally, the process of recovery from trauma is unique to each individual, and may involve a wide range of different experiences, challenges, and opportunities for growth.

One of the most important factors in facilitating recovery from trauma is the presence of a supportive and non-judgmental environment. This may involve working with a therapist or counsellor who is experienced in trauma-informed care, or connecting with others who have experienced similar traumas and challenges.

Research suggests that social support is a critical factor in promoting recovery from trauma, and can involve a range of different activities and interactions, including:

  • Talking to others about one's experiences and emotions
  • Receiving emotional support and validation from others
  • Engaging in social activities and connecting with others
  • Participating in group therapy or support groups

It's also worth noting that recovery from trauma is not a one-time event, but rather a ongoing process that requires continued effort, commitment, and support. Individuals may need to work through their recovery over the course of weeks, months, or even years, and may require ongoing support and guidance from therapists, counsellors, and other healthcare professionals.

Ultimately, the process of recovery from trauma is a testament to the human capacity for resilience, growth, and transformation. By acknowledging the reality of our traumatic experiences, taking responsibility for our own healing and recovery, and working through the emotions, thoughts, and bodily sensations associated with trauma, we can emerge from the recovery process with a renewed sense of hope, purpose, and direction.

As individuals work through their recovery, they may also need to develop new coping strategies and skills, such as:

  • Grounding techniques, such as deep breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, or sensory exercises
  • Emotional regulation strategies, such as mindfulness, self-compassion, or cognitive-behavioural therapy
  • Self-care activities, such as exercise, meditation, or creative pursuits

These strategies can help individuals manage their symptoms, regulate their emotions, and develop a greater sense of control and agency over their lives.

Finally, it's worth noting that recovery from trauma is not a solo endeavour, but rather a collaborative process that involves working with others, including therapists, counsellors, and healthcare professionals. By seeking out support and guidance, individuals can access the resources and expertise they need to navigate the complex and often challenging process of recovery from trauma.

FAQs

Fight, flight, freeze and fawn questions answered

What is the fight, flight, freeze and fawn response?

The fight, flight, freeze and fawn response refers to a set of automatic reactions the body undergoes in response to perceived threats or stressors. These responses are typically triggered by the body's 'fight or flight' system, which prepares the body to either confront or flee from danger. However, when the threat is too great, the body may also experience a 'freeze' response, where it becomes immobile and unable to respond. In some cases, the body may also exhibit a 'fawn' response, where it attempts to appease the threat by being overly accommodating or submissive.

What triggers the fight response?

The fight response is typically triggered by a perceived threat that requires the individual to stand up for themselves or assert their power. This can be in response to physical aggression, verbal abuse, or other forms of intimidation. When the body perceives a threat, it releases stress hormones such as adrenaline and cortisol, which prepare the muscles for action and increase heart rate and blood pressure. The individual may also experience a surge in energy and a sense of heightened alertness.

What triggers the flight response?

The flight response is typically triggered by a perceived threat that requires the individual to escape or flee from danger. This can be in response to physical danger, such as a natural disaster or an attack, or emotional danger, such as a toxic relationship or a traumatic event. When the body perceives a threat, it releases stress hormones such as adrenaline and cortisol, which prepare the muscles for action and increase heart rate and blood pressure. The individual may also experience a sense of panic and a strong desire to escape the situation.

What triggers the freeze response?

The freeze response is typically triggered by a perceived threat that is too great for the individual to cope with. This can be in response to physical or emotional trauma, or a combination of both. When the body perceives a threat that is too great, it may become overwhelmed and unable to respond. The individual may experience a sense of numbness or disconnection from their body and emotions, and may become immobile or unable to move.

What triggers the fawn response?

The fawn response is typically triggered by a perceived threat that requires the individual to appease or placate the threat. This can be in response to emotional abuse, manipulation, or other forms of exploitation. When the body perceives a threat, it may release stress hormones such as oxytocin and vasopressin, which promote feelings of attachment and bonding. The individual may experience a strong desire to please or appease the threat, and may become overly accommodating or submissive in an attempt to avoid conflict.

Can people experience multiple responses at once?

Yes, people can experience multiple responses at once. For example, someone who is being physically threatened may experience a fight response, while also feeling a sense of freeze or numbness due to the overwhelming nature of the threat. Similarly, someone who is being emotionally manipulated may experience a fawn response, while also feeling a sense of anger or resentment. It's not uncommon for people to experience multiple responses simultaneously, and it's often a reflection of the complexity and nuance of human emotions.
