Trauma and dissociation · Complete guide

What Is PTSD?

Post traumatic stress disorder can develop after exposure to traumatic events and may involve re-experiencing, avoidance, continuing threat responses and significant disruption to everyday life.

Understanding trauma responses without defining a person by what happened

Clear answer

Post traumatic stress disorder, usually shortened to PTSD, is a recognised trauma-related mental health condition that can develop after exposure to death, threatened death, serious injury, sexual violence or other profoundly traumatic events. Its central features include re-experiencing the trauma in the present, avoidance of reminders and a continuing sense of threat.

PTSD is more than feeling understandably distressed after something terrible happens. Symptoms persist, cause significant impairment and organise experience around a danger that the brain and body continue to treat as present. The condition can affect sleep, concentration, relationships, physical health, work and a person’s confidence in their own safety.

Not everyone who experiences trauma develops PTSD. People can have severe and valid distress without meeting the diagnostic criteria, while somebody who appears outwardly capable may be living with significant symptoms. Diagnosis requires an individual assessment rather than comparison with another person’s trauma.

Definition

What does PTSD mean?

PTSD describes a pattern in which traumatic danger continues to shape present experience after the event or events have ended. Memories may return with the sensory and emotional force of the original situation. Reminders are avoided, and the threat system remains prepared for further harm.

The World Health Organization includes PTSD in ICD-11 among disorders specifically associated with stress. The ICD-11 framework emphasises three central symptom groups: re-experiencing in the present, deliberate avoidance and a persistent perception of heightened current threat.

Other classification systems describe a broader collection of symptoms, including negative changes in mood and thinking. Clinical terminology may therefore vary between services. The purpose of diagnosis is to guide suitable care, not to determine whether somebody’s experience was serious enough to deserve compassion.

The word disorder can feel stigmatising. It does not mean that the person’s responses appeared without reason. Many PTSD symptoms are understandable survival processes that remain active beyond the situation in which they were needed. Understanding this origin can reduce shame while still recognising that treatment may be necessary.

Traumatic exposure

What kinds of experiences may precede PTSD?

PTSD can follow direct exposure to traumatic events, witnessing serious harm, learning that severe trauma affected somebody close or repeated professional exposure to disturbing details. The exact definitions used in diagnostic systems matter during assessment, but the absence of a PTSD diagnosis does not make other forms of suffering unimportant.

Serious accidents

Road collisions, workplace incidents, fires, falls and other accidents may create intense fear, injury, helplessness or exposure to death.

Violence and assault

Physical assault, sexual violence, domestic abuse, robbery, captivity and threats can produce enduring trauma responses.

War and persecution

Combat, torture, displacement, terrorism, imprisonment and witnessing atrocities may involve repeated and complex traumatic exposure.

Medical trauma

Life-threatening illness, emergency treatment, intensive care, traumatic birth and invasive procedures may be experienced as overwhelming danger.

Disasters

Floods, earthquakes, explosions and other disasters can combine immediate danger with bereavement, displacement and continuing uncertainty.

Occupational exposure

Emergency workers, military personnel and others may encounter traumatic events repeatedly through their work.

Trauma can be acute or repeated. PTSD may follow a single incident, while complex PTSD is more strongly associated with prolonged, repeated or difficult-to-escape experiences and includes additional difficulties involving emotional regulation, self-concept and relationships.

After trauma

Normal early reactions compared with PTSD

In the days and weeks after trauma, many people experience shock, intrusive images, disrupted sleep, emotional numbness, tearfulness, irritability, startle responses or difficulty concentrating. These reactions can be intense without necessarily developing into PTSD.

For some people, symptoms reduce as safety returns, practical problems are addressed and the experience becomes integrated into autobiographical memory. For others, symptoms remain severe, become more organised or emerge after an initial period of apparent stability.

PTSD is considered when the pattern persists and causes clinically significant distress or impairment. Timing requirements differ between diagnostic systems and services. An individual does not need to delay seeking help simply because a particular number of weeks has not passed.

Early support should not force detailed retelling. Practical safety, sleep, social support, accurate information and choice can be more valuable than pressuring somebody to process the entire event immediately.

Core symptom

Re-experiencing trauma in the present

Re-experiencing is not the same as choosing to think about a painful memory. The event may intrude with a present-tense quality, as though the danger is returning now. The person’s body can react before they consciously recognise the reminder.

Flashbacks vary. Some involve vivid visual scenes; others are dominated by sounds, smells, bodily sensations, terror, shame or helplessness. Awareness of the present may remain partly intact or become significantly reduced.

Nightmares may reproduce an event directly or repeat its emotional themes. Somebody who was trapped may dream about pursuit or confinement without replaying the exact setting. Waking can involve panic, disorientation, sweating and difficulty returning to sleep.

Intrusive memories may appear suddenly during ordinary activities. A smell, expression, date, location, news report or physical sensation can activate the memory network. The trigger is not always consciously obvious.

In ICD-11, re-experiencing requires more than remembering the event with sadness. It involves reliving in the present through vivid intrusive memories, flashbacks or nightmares accompanied by strong emotion or bodily sensation.

Core symptom

Avoidance of reminders

Avoidance is an understandable attempt to prevent overwhelming distress. A person may avoid places, people, conversations, media, activities, sensations or thoughts associated with the trauma.

Internal avoidance can include suppressing memories, disconnecting from emotion, remaining constantly busy, using substances or refusing sleep because nightmares feel intolerable. External avoidance might involve changing routes, abandoning work, avoiding healthcare or withdrawing from relationships.

Avoidance often provides immediate relief. That relief reinforces the behaviour, making avoidance more likely next time. Over time, life becomes narrower and the person receives fewer opportunities to learn that some reminders can be encountered safely.

This does not mean somebody should confront every reminder without preparation. Effective treatment uses a collaborative and paced approach. Exposure that overwhelms the person or ignores continuing danger is not therapeutic simply because it involves facing fear.

Core symptom

A continuing sense of current threat

After trauma, the nervous system may remain organised around rapid danger detection. The person can understand intellectually that an event has ended while their body continues to anticipate repetition.

Hypervigilance may involve scanning exits, watching faces, checking locks, monitoring noises or positioning oneself where the whole room is visible. Small changes in another person’s tone may receive intense attention because similar cues once preceded harm.

An exaggerated startle response can cause sudden movement, touch or noise to trigger a strong physical reaction. Sleep may remain light because deeper rest feels unsafe. Irritability can increase when the body is exhausted and continually mobilised.

A sensitive alarm system favours false positives over missed danger. From a survival perspective, reacting to ten harmless sounds may once have been less costly than ignoring one genuine threat. Problems develop when this strategy continues to dominate safe environments.

Wider presentation

Other symptoms that may accompany PTSD

People with PTSD may experience guilt, shame, grief, anger, emotional numbing, reduced interest, social withdrawal and difficulty imagining a future. Depression and anxiety commonly coexist.

Concentration can become difficult because attention repeatedly shifts towards threat or intrusive memory. Decision-making may feel exhausting. Ordinary mistakes can trigger disproportionate self-criticism when the person already feels unsafe or defective.

Dissociation may occur, including depersonalisation, derealisation or gaps in awareness. Physical symptoms can include headaches, muscle tension, digestive disturbance, fatigue, pain, palpitations and disrupted sleep. Medical symptoms still require appropriate assessment rather than automatic attribution to trauma.

Some people use alcohol, medication or recreational drugs to reduce nightmares, arousal or emotional pain. Short-term relief can develop into dependence, worsen sleep and interfere with trauma treatment. Support should address the function of substance use without moral judgement.

Risk-taking, aggression or self-harm can occur but are not inevitable features of PTSD. Media stereotypes that portray everyone with PTSD as violent are inaccurate and harmful. Many people direct distress inward or work hard to conceal symptoms.

Brain and body

Why the body reacts before conscious thought

Threat detection operates through interacting brain and body systems. Sensory processing, attention, memory, stress hormones and autonomic arousal contribute to rapid decisions about whether action is required.

Protective responses include fight, flight, freeze, submit, appease and dissociation. These are not rigid personality types. The same person may show different responses depending on the perceived danger, available escape routes, past learning and relationship context.

During a trigger, conscious reasoning may temporarily become less accessible while protective systems prioritise speed. Telling somebody simply to calm down can therefore be ineffective. Regulation often begins with orientation, reduced stimulation, bodily support and enough time for arousal to change.

Understanding physiology does not remove responsibility for harmful actions. It explains why choice may narrow under threat and identifies places where treatment can expand awareness, regulation and alternative responses.

Trauma memory

Why traumatic memories may feel different

Ordinary autobiographical memories are usually recognised as belonging to the past. Traumatic memories may be poorly contextualised, dominated by sensory fragments or easily activated by reminders that resemble part of the original danger.

A flashback can therefore feel present even when the person knows the date and location. The body responds to the activated memory network rather than waiting for a deliberate conclusion about current safety.

Memory after trauma can be fragmented without being completely absent. Sequence and peripheral details may remain unclear, while central sensations or meanings are vivid. Inconsistency in minor details does not by itself prove dishonesty, but neither does emotional intensity guarantee complete accuracy.

Ethical therapy helps distinguish past from present and integrate memory without treating every image as literal evidence or forcing recall. Clinical care and legal investigation require different standards and should not be conflated.

Risk and protection

Why do some people develop PTSD while others do not?

PTSD is not determined by strength, courage or moral character. Two people can experience the same event and have different outcomes because risk develops through many interacting factors.

The intensity, duration and interpersonal nature of trauma can influence risk. Events involving severe helplessness, injury, sexual violence, betrayal, repeated exposure or threat from somebody trusted may have especially profound effects.

Previous trauma can increase vulnerability, particularly when earlier experiences remain unresolved or occurred during childhood. Existing anxiety, depression, dissociation or limited social support may also affect recovery. These are risk factors rather than predictions.

What happens after trauma matters greatly. Continuing danger, financial instability, homelessness, legal conflict, disbelief and repeated exposure to reminders may prevent the nervous system from recognising that the event has ended.

Supportive relationships, practical safety, access to healthcare and opportunities to regain meaningful control may be protective. Protection does not guarantee that symptoms will not develop, and developing PTSD does not mean support failed.

Timing

Can PTSD begin months or years after trauma?

Some people experience symptoms immediately. Others have a delayed presentation in which the full diagnostic pattern becomes apparent months or years later. This does not necessarily mean there were no earlier effects.

Initial survival demands may require somebody to remain highly functional. Military personnel, emergency workers, parents and people escaping abuse may focus on immediate responsibilities while emotional processing is postponed.

Symptoms can become more visible when life becomes safer, when another stressful event reduces coping capacity or when a reminder activates previously manageable memories. Retirement, illness, bereavement, parenthood and anniversaries can change how earlier experiences are understood.

Delayed symptoms should still receive proper assessment. A temporal connection with past trauma may be important, but clinicians should also consider current stressors, depression, anxiety, physical illness and other possible explanations.

Diagnosis

How is PTSD professionally assessed?

A PTSD assessment considers the traumatic exposure, the nature and duration of symptoms, functional impairment, current safety and alternative explanations. It should be collaborative and should not require unnecessary graphic detail at the first appointment.

The clinician may ask about intrusive memories, flashbacks, nightmares, avoidance, hypervigilance, startle responses, sleep, concentration, mood and dissociation. They may also explore depression, substance use, self-harm, physical symptoms and the person’s support network.

Assessment should establish whether danger is genuinely over. Hypervigilance in a currently unsafe environment may be adaptive. Treatment planning is different when abuse, persecution, occupational exposure or another threat continues.

Validated questionnaires can help measure symptoms and monitor change, but a score is not a complete diagnosis. The person’s history, context and functioning remain essential.

Physical health should not be overlooked. Palpitations, pain, dizziness, fatigue and sleep disturbance can accompany PTSD but may also require medical investigation. Trauma-informed healthcare avoids both dismissing symptoms and subjecting the person to unnecessary procedures without explanation.

Comparison

PTSD compared with complex PTSD

Area PTSD Complex PTSD
Core symptoms Re-experiencing in the present, avoidance and a continuing sense of threat. Includes the same three core PTSD symptom groups.
Emotional regulation Distress, anger, fear and numbing may occur. Persistent and pervasive difficulty regulating emotion forms an additional central feature.
Sense of self Trauma may alter beliefs about oneself and the world. Enduring worthlessness, shame, defeat or a deeply negative self-concept is characteristic.
Relationships Avoidance, withdrawal and mistrust may affect connection. Persistent difficulties sustaining closeness, trust and relational safety are central.
Trauma pattern Can follow a single incident or repeated traumatic events. More strongly associated with prolonged, repeated or difficult-to-escape trauma.

Complex PTSD is not simply “more severe PTSD”, and the diagnoses should not be used to rank suffering. Somebody with PTSD following one event may be profoundly impaired, while another person with prolonged trauma may not meet the criteria for either condition.

Differential understanding

Conditions that may overlap with PTSD

Depression

Withdrawal, poor sleep, hopelessness and reduced concentration overlap, but PTSD requires its characteristic trauma-related pattern.

Panic disorder

Sudden fear and physical arousal may resemble triggered trauma responses. Assessment considers whether attacks are linked to trauma reminders.

Generalised anxiety

Persistent worry may coexist with PTSD, but generalised anxiety is not defined by re-experiencing a traumatic event.

Obsessive compulsive disorder

Intrusive thoughts and avoidance can appear similar, although obsessions and compulsions have a different functional pattern.

Dissociative disorders

Depersonalisation, derealisation and amnesia may accompany PTSD or form part of a separate dissociative condition.

Traumatic brain injury

Head injury can produce concentration, memory, sleep and emotional difficulties that overlap with PTSD.

Prolonged grief

Grief and PTSD can coexist after traumatic bereavement, but yearning and loss remain distinct from threat-based re-experiencing.

Adjustment disorder

Significant stress reactions may follow difficult events without meeting the trauma exposure or symptom requirements for PTSD.

More than one diagnosis may be appropriate. Differential assessment is not an attempt to minimise trauma; it helps identify the most suitable treatment and any additional support needs.

Daily life

How PTSD can affect relationships

PTSD can make closeness feel both necessary and unsafe. The person may want reassurance while also feeling crowded, misunderstood or unable to explain their internal experience.

Emotional numbing may be misinterpreted as lack of love. Irritability may create conflict, while avoidance can lead somebody to withdraw from shared activities. Sleep disruption affects both the individual and their household.

Partners and relatives may become overly watchful, take responsibility for preventing every trigger or avoid discussing important concerns. Although understandable, this can gradually narrow everybody’s life.

Helpful relationships combine compassion with clear boundaries. Supporters can ask what helps during a flashback, communicate plans predictably and encourage treatment without becoming the person’s therapist.

Repair after conflict matters. A trauma response may explain why somebody reacted strongly, but it does not automatically excuse intimidation or harm. Safety and accountability can coexist with trauma-informed understanding.
