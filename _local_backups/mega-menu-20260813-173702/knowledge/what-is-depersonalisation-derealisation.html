Dissociation · In-depth guide

What Are Depersonalisation and Derealisation?

Depersonalisation can make you feel detached from yourself. Derealisation can make the world feel unreal, distant or dreamlike. This guide explains these experiences, why they happen and how recovery may be supported.

Understanding detachment while remaining connected with present reality

Clear answer

Depersonalisation is a sense of detachment from yourself, while derealisation is a sense that the world around you is unreal, distant, dreamlike or unfamiliar. They are forms of dissociation and can occur briefly during intense stress, panic, exhaustion or trauma. When they are persistent, recurrent and significantly distressing, a clinician may consider depersonalisation-derealisation disorder.

People experiencing depersonalisation or derealisation usually retain awareness that the feeling of unreality is an internal experience. The world may feel unreal, but the person generally understands that it has not literally ceased to be real. This preserved reality testing is an important distinction from some psychotic experiences.

Meaning

What are depersonalisation and derealisation?

Depersonalisation and derealisation describe disruptions in the ordinary sense of being present, embodied and connected with reality. They often occur together, but either can be more prominent.

During depersonalisation, somebody may feel detached from their thoughts, emotions, voice, body or actions. They may describe observing themselves from a distance, functioning automatically or feeling as though there is a barrier between their awareness and their experience.

During derealisation, the external environment may feel artificial, distant, flat, visually altered, foggy or dreamlike. Familiar surroundings can seem unfamiliar. Sounds may feel unusually loud, quiet or distant, and time may appear slowed, accelerated or fragmented.

These experiences can be intensely frightening, but the sensations themselves do not mean that the person is “going mad”, losing their personality or permanently disconnected from reality.

Key distinction

Depersonalisation compared with derealisation

Experience Depersonalisation Derealisation
Main focus Detachment from oneself, one’s body, emotions, thoughts or actions. Detachment from the external environment, people, objects or surroundings.
Common descriptions “I feel outside myself”, “I am on autopilot” or “my body does not feel like mine”. “The world looks unreal”, “everything seems foggy” or “people seem distant”.
Emotional experience Emotions may feel muted, absent, distant or disconnected from the person. The environment may appear emotionally flat, unfamiliar or lacking its ordinary sense of meaning.
Reality testing The person usually recognises that this is a feeling of detachment rather than literal separation from the body. The person usually recognises that the environment feels unreal rather than believing it has literally become unreal.

A person can move between both experiences. The distinction helps describe the symptoms but does not imply two completely separate conditions.

Symptoms

What may depersonalisation and derealisation feel like?

Watching yourself

You may feel like an observer watching your actions, speech or movements rather than fully initiating them.

Emotional numbness

Love, sadness, excitement or fear may seem distant even when you know intellectually that you care.

Body unfamiliarity

Your hands, face, voice, reflection or whole body may feel strange, altered or disconnected from your sense of self.

Dreamlike surroundings

Rooms, streets and people may seem unreal, artificial, two-dimensional, foggy or separated by an invisible barrier.

Altered perception

Distance, size, colour, light, sound or time may feel subtly distorted without a loss of awareness that perception has changed.

Automatic functioning

You may complete ordinary tasks competently while feeling emotionally absent or as though your body is operating automatically.

The experiences may last seconds, minutes or hours, or occur repeatedly over longer periods. Some people experience a persistent background sense of unreality with episodes of greater intensity.

Causes and triggers

Why can these experiences happen?

Dissociation may operate as a protective response when emotional or physical arousal becomes overwhelming. By reducing the intensity or immediacy of experience, detachment may allow somebody to continue functioning during circumstances that feel impossible to process normally.

Depersonalisation and derealisation may be associated with severe stress, panic attacks, traumatic experiences, prolonged anxiety, depression, sleep deprivation or emotional overload. They may also occur after using cannabis, hallucinogens or other substances, during withdrawal, or alongside some physical and neurological conditions.

Not every episode is caused by trauma. Assuming trauma is always responsible can obscure panic, medication effects, substance use, migraine, seizure disorders or other explanations requiring appropriate assessment.

People sometimes become trapped in a feedback loop: an unfamiliar sensation creates fear; fear increases body monitoring; monitoring makes the sensation more noticeable; and the increased sense of unreality produces more fear.

Important comparison

Is derealisation the same as psychosis?

No. During depersonalisation or derealisation, the person commonly knows that the experience is a disturbing feeling or alteration in perception. They may repeatedly check whether things are real, but they recognise that the world has not literally transformed.

Psychosis can involve hallucinations, delusions or a substantial disruption in testing what is real. However, experiences do not always fit simple categories, and dissociation and psychotic symptoms can coexist. Somebody experiencing voices, fixed unusual beliefs, severe confusion or rapidly deteriorating functioning should receive professional assessment.

The distinction should not be used to dismiss distress. Preserved reality testing does not make chronic depersonalisation or derealisation minor; the symptoms can substantially affect concentration, relationships, work and quality of life.

Anxiety cycle

How panic and checking can maintain unreality

When somebody fears that detachment means permanent brain damage or a complete loss of control, they may continually inspect their vision, thoughts, emotions and bodily sensations. This inward monitoring increases awareness of every ordinary fluctuation.

Repeatedly asking “Do I feel real yet?” can unintentionally keep attention fixed on the symptom. Online searching, mirror checking and repeated reassurance may briefly reduce fear but reinforce the idea that the sensation is dangerous.

Treatment may therefore involve understanding the symptoms, reducing catastrophic interpretations, gradually returning attention to meaningful activity and addressing the anxiety or trauma processes contributing to the experience.

Assessment

How are persistent symptoms assessed?

A clinician may ask when the symptoms began, how long they last, what makes them stronger or weaker, and how they affect daily functioning. Assessment should consider panic, depression, PTSD, complex PTSD, other dissociative symptoms, psychosis, sleep, medication, alcohol and drug use.

Physical and neurological causes may need investigation, particularly when symptoms began suddenly, follow a head injury, include loss of consciousness, occur with seizures or severe headaches, or are accompanied by new neurological changes.

Depersonalisation-derealisation disorder is considered when symptoms are persistent or recurrent, cause significant distress or impairment and are not better explained by substances, another medical condition or another mental disorder. A website or symptom checklist cannot make this determination.

Treatment

How are depersonalisation and derealisation treated?

Treatment depends on the person’s complete presentation. Talking therapies are commonly recommended for dissociative disorders. Therapy may address fear of the sensations, avoidance, excessive self-monitoring, traumatic experiences, panic, depression and difficulties regulating arousal.

Cognitive behavioural approaches may help identify catastrophic interpretations and reduce cycles of checking and avoidance. Where trauma is relevant, trauma treatment should be paced carefully and adapted to the person’s stability and level of dissociation.

There is no single medicine that directly cures depersonalisation or derealisation. Medication may sometimes be prescribed for associated anxiety or depression. Benefits, risks and side effects should be discussed with a qualified prescriber.

Recovery may involve symptoms becoming less frequent, less frightening or less central to attention. Some people improve gradually rather than experiencing a single moment when everything suddenly feels normal.

Grounding

What may help during an episode?

  • Name the date, location and activity you are currently doing.
  • Notice several neutral external details without repeatedly testing whether they look real.
  • Feel the support of the chair or floor and describe the physical contact accurately.
  • Use steady breathing without forcing unusually deep breaths, which can worsen light-headedness.
  • Continue a safe, familiar activity rather than stopping everything to monitor the sensation.
  • Reduce caffeine, recreational drugs and sleep disruption where they are contributing factors.
  • Use a brief statement such as “This is a recognised dissociative sensation, and it can pass”.

Highly intense sensory grounding is not suitable for everyone. If a technique increases panic or disconnection, stop and try a gentler form of present-moment orientation.

Supporting somebody

How can friends and family help?

Listen without arguing about how the experience “should” feel. Calmly acknowledge that the sensation is frightening while avoiding statements that confirm the world is literally unreal.

Ask whether the person wants conversation, quiet company, a familiar activity or help contacting professional support. Avoid repeatedly testing their memory or perception. Predictability, ordinary conversation and patient reassurance can be more useful than dramatic intervention.

If symptoms are persistent, encourage a GP or mental health assessment. Supporters should also maintain their own boundaries and seek advice when the level of distress becomes difficult to manage.

Seeking help

When should somebody obtain urgent support?

Seek urgent assessment when detachment follows a head injury, is accompanied by seizures, weakness, loss of consciousness, severe confusion or other sudden neurological symptoms. Immediate help is also necessary when somebody cannot remain safe, is at risk of harm or is experiencing suicidal thoughts.

In the UK, call 999 or attend A&E for immediate danger. For urgent mental health help that is not an emergency, contact NHS 111 and select the mental health option where available. Samaritans can be contacted free on 116 123.

Frequently asked questions

Questions about depersonalisation and derealisation

Can anxiety cause derealisation?

Yes. Derealisation can occur during intense anxiety or panic, particularly when arousal, rapid breathing and threat monitoring become severe.

Does derealisation mean I am developing psychosis?

Not usually. People experiencing derealisation commonly recognise that the world feels unreal rather than believing it has literally become unreal. Professional assessment is appropriate when symptoms are unclear or include hallucinations, fixed unusual beliefs or severe confusion.

Can cannabis trigger depersonalisation?

Yes. Cannabis and other substances can trigger or intensify depersonalisation and derealisation in some people. Persistent symptoms should be discussed honestly with a healthcare professional.

Can the symptoms become permanent?

Symptoms can persist or recur, but many people improve with appropriate treatment, reduced fear, better regulation and attention to contributing conditions.

Should I keep checking whether I feel real?

Constant checking may keep attention fixed on the symptom and maintain anxiety. A therapist can help somebody reduce checking safely without dismissing their distress.

Can depersonalisation affect emotions?

Yes. Emotions may feel muted or distant. This does not necessarily mean the person has stopped caring or lost the capacity to feel.
