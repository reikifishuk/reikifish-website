Complete guide · Emotional health

What is empathy?

Empathy is not a single feeling or a mysterious gift. It is a collection of emotional and cognitive processes that help us notice, understand and sometimes share another person's experience while recognising that their inner world remains separate from our own.

Prepared by Andy Fish · ReikiFish Knowledge Base · Evidence led psychology education

Quick answer

Empathy means understanding another person's experience

Empathy is the capacity to recognise, understand or emotionally resonate with another person's feelings and perspective while maintaining a distinction between their experience and your own.

It can involve cognitive empathy, which helps us understand what somebody may be thinking or feeling, and affective empathy, which involves emotionally responding to or sharing aspects of that feeling.

Empathy does not automatically produce kindness, agreement or accurate understanding. It works best when combined with curiosity, emotional regulation, compassion, boundaries and a willingness to check assumptions.

A multidimensional capacity

Empathy is more than “putting yourself in someone else's shoes”

The familiar phrase is useful, but incomplete. We cannot literally enter another person's mind. We interpret their words, expression, tone, behaviour and context using our own nervous system, memories and expectations.

Empathy therefore contains both connection and uncertainty. We may come close to understanding another person's experience, but responsible empathy remembers that the other person remains the best available source on what they actually feel.

01

Perceiving

Noticing emotional and social information in facial expression, voice, language, posture, behaviour and context.

02

Interpreting another person's experience

Making a reasoned inference about another person's perspective, motives, needs or emotional state.

03

Resonating

Experiencing an emotional response that corresponds in some way to what another person appears to feel.

04

Differentiating

Remembering that the feeling belongs to the other person and distinguishing their experience from your own reaction.

Types and components

Cognitive empathy, affective empathy and empathic concern

Understanding

Cognitive empathy

Cognitive empathy is the ability to understand another person's perspective or infer their emotional and mental state. It overlaps with perspective taking, mentalising and theory of mind.

It allows someone to recognise why another person is afraid, angry or hurt even if they do not emotionally share that feeling.

Emotional resonance

Affective empathy

Affective empathy is an emotional response to another person's state. Seeing distress may produce a corresponding feeling of sadness, concern or bodily discomfort.

Healthy affective empathy retains self and other distinction rather than becoming complete emotional fusion.

Care and motivation

Empathic concern

Empathic concern involves warmth, care and concern for another person's welfare. It is often associated with a desire to support rather than merely understand.

Accuracy

Empathic accuracy

Empathic accuracy concerns how closely a person's interpretation matches what the other person reports thinking or feeling. Feeling empathic is not the same as being accurate.

People can have uneven empathy profiles. Someone may understand emotions accurately without sharing them strongly, or feel another person's distress intensely while misunderstanding its cause.

Important distinctions

Empathy, sympathy, compassion and emotional contagion

Process Central experience Possible outcome Important limitation
Cognitive empathy “I understand what you may be experiencing.” Perspective taking and more informed communication. Understanding does not guarantee care or moral behaviour.
Affective empathy “I feel an emotional response to your experience.” Connection, concern and motivation to respond. Strong resonance can become personal distress.
Sympathy “I feel sorry for what you are experiencing.” Concern and acknowledgement. It may involve emotional distance or pity.
Compassion “I care about your suffering and want to help.” Supportive, prosocial action. Help must still respect autonomy and boundaries.
Emotional contagion Automatically catching another person's emotion. Rapid social coordination or shared mood. It may occur without understanding whose emotion it is.
Personal distress Becoming overwhelmed by another person's suffering. Withdrawal, avoidance or attempts to stop personal discomfort. The reaction becomes centred on the observer rather than the person in need.

How empathy happens

From noticing emotion to responding well

Attention

Empathy begins by noticing the person. Distraction, stress, prejudice and competing goals can reduce attention before interpretation even begins.

Perception

The observer detects verbal and non-verbal information, but cues can be ambiguous, culturally shaped or deliberately concealed.

Interpretation

The mind forms a model of what the person may feel and why. Memory, assumptions and personal experience influence this model.

Emotional resonance

The observer may experience a corresponding emotional or bodily response, although its intensity varies substantially between people and situations.

Self and other distinction

Regulation helps the observer recognise: “This reaction is happening in me, but the original experience belongs to them.”

Checking

Empathy becomes more accurate when interpretations are offered tentatively and checked rather than presented as certainty.

Response

Compassion, values, boundaries and practical judgement shape whether understanding becomes listening, support, protection, respectful challenge or no action.

Empathic accuracy

Why feeling another person's pain does not mean reading their mind

People regularly confuse confidence with accuracy. A person may say, “I know exactly how you feel,” while projecting their own history onto someone whose experience is different.

Accuracy improves when empathy is treated as a hypothesis rather than a supernatural certainty. Useful language includes:

  • “It sounds as though you felt dismissed. Have I understood that correctly?”
  • “I may be wrong, but I wondered whether that left you feeling unsafe.”
  • “What part of this has been hardest for you?”
  • “Would you like me to listen, help you think it through or offer practical support?”

These responses combine empathy with intellectual humility. They invite correction and preserve the other person's authority over their own experience.

Brain and body

There is no single “empathy centre” in the brain

Neuroscience supports the view that empathy is multidimensional. Research associates affective sharing with networks that include the anterior insula and anterior cingulate regions, while perspective taking and mentalising more strongly involve medial prefrontal and temporoparietal systems.

These networks overlap and interact. Brain imaging identifies patterns across groups; it cannot reveal whether one individual is caring, moral or accurately empathic in everyday life.

Important neuroscience caution

A colourful brain scan is not a personality diagnosis

Neural activation varies with the task, relationship, emotional intensity, attention and research method. Imaging findings should not be used to declare that a particular person “has no empathy”.

Development

How empathy develops

Early emotional responsiveness appears before mature perspective taking. As language, executive functioning, emotion recognition and theory of mind develop, children become better able to understand that another person can hold feelings, knowledge and beliefs different from their own.

Development is influenced by interacting factors, including:

  • Temperament and biological sensitivity.
  • Language and emotion vocabulary.
  • Secure, responsive caregiving.
  • Opportunities to repair conflict and consider consequences.
  • Culture and family norms around emotional expression.
  • Executive functioning and impulse control.
  • Stress, trauma, safety and emotional regulation.
  • Social learning from peers, adults and media.

Empathy continues to change throughout life. It is neither completely fixed nor determined by one childhood experience.

Selective empathy

Empathy is influenced by identity, similarity and bias

Empathy is not distributed evenly. People often find it easier to empathise with those they recognise as similar, familiar, likeable, innocent or part of their own group.

Competition, threat, dehumanisation and strong group identity can reduce empathic responding towards outsiders. This means that empathy alone is not a reliable foundation for justice: it may be strongest for vivid individuals and weakest for distant or disliked groups.

Similarity

“They are like me”

Shared experience can improve understanding, but it can also encourage projection and the assumption that another person must feel exactly as we did.

Group membership

Ingroup preference

People may respond more strongly to suffering within their own social, political, cultural or family group.

Likeability

Deservingness judgements

Empathy may decline when a person is blamed for their situation or seen as unlikeable, even when their suffering remains real.

Visibility

The identifiable person

One vivid story can provoke more feeling than a large anonymous group, although the larger harm may require greater attention.

Capacity is not character

Empathy does not automatically make somebody kind

Cognitive empathy may help a person comfort, negotiate or cooperate. The same understanding can also be used to anticipate vulnerability, persuade, deceive or control.

What matters is how empathic information is used. Compassion, honesty, responsibility, fairness and respect for autonomy are separate moral and behavioural qualities.

Understanding is not agreement. You can understand why somebody behaved as they did without approving of the behaviour, excusing harm or abandoning your boundaries.

Empathy and personality

Narcissism, psychopathy and the myth of “zero empathy”

Empathic functioning can be compromised in several psychological conditions and personality patterns, but blanket claims are misleading. Research distinguishes between capacity, motivation, emotional resonance and performance in a particular situation.

Reviews of narcissistic personality disorder describe empathy as variable and compromised rather than universally absent. A person may understand another's perspective in one setting but disengage when shame, rivalry, entitlement or self-protection becomes activated.

Research often associates psychopathic traits more strongly with reduced affective empathy, while aspects of cognitive understanding may remain available. These are group-level findings and cannot diagnose an individual.

What about the “dark empath”?

“Dark empath” is not a clinical diagnosis. Some research has identified statistical profiles combining elevated dark personality traits with self-reported empathy, but this does not establish a distinct disorder or a special class of person.

The popular claim that a dark empath can psychically detect every weakness exaggerates what empathy is. Strategic perspective taking, social observation and manipulation should not be confused with compassionate emotional understanding.

Read the fuller guides to narcissism and Narcissistic Personality Disorder.

Empathy with stability

Why healthy empathy needs emotional boundaries

Empathy does not require absorbing another person's emotions, becoming permanently available or taking responsibility for solving their life.

Without self and other distinction, emotional resonance can become personal distress. The observer may become overwhelmed, withdraw, over-function or try to control the other person's choices to reduce their own discomfort.

Disconnection

Too little engagement

Emotion is ignored, dismissed or intellectualised without meaningful attention.

Recognition

Understanding without emotional fusion

The person's perspective is heard and considered without assuming complete knowledge.

Compassion

Connected response

Understanding is combined with care, regulation and appropriate support.

Overidentification

Emotional fusion

The observer loses perspective, absorbs distress or assumes responsibility for the other person.

Healthy boundaries allow someone to remain present without being consumed. See the full guide to healthy boundaries.

Empathic distress and burnout

Does caring too much cause burnout?

The phrase “empathy fatigue” can oversimplify the evidence. Burnout is influenced by workload, organisational culture, control, resources, exposure to suffering, personal distress, recovery and support.

Research often finds different relationships for different components of empathy. Perspective taking and empathic concern may be associated with positive professional functioning, while unregulated personal distress is more consistently associated with emotional exhaustion and withdrawal.

The answer is not necessarily to care less. It may be to develop regulation, realistic responsibility, compassionate concern, supervision, recovery and workable limits.

Practical development

How to practise more accurate and sustainable empathy

01

Listen for meaning

Notice emotion, need and context rather than listening only to prepare a reply.

02

Use tentative language

Say “It sounds as though…” instead of claiming certainty about another person's mind.

03

Ask what support means

Do not assume advice is wanted. Ask whether the person needs listening, ideas, practical help or space.

04

Expand emotional vocabulary

Greater emotional precision helps distinguish fear from shame, anger from hurt and sadness from helplessness.

05

Regulate before responding

Slow down when another person's emotion activates panic, defensiveness or an urge to fix everything.

06

Check projection

Ask whether your interpretation comes from their evidence or from what a similar event meant to you.

07

Seek unfamiliar perspectives

Carefully chosen stories and conversations can challenge narrow assumptions and ingroup bias.

08

Pair empathy with boundaries

Care without promising what you cannot sustainably or safely provide.

Research and measurement

How psychologists study empathy

Empathy can be measured through self-report questionnaires, emotion-recognition tasks, perspective-taking tests, observed behaviour, reports from other people, physiological measures and brain imaging.

Each method measures something slightly different. Self-reports may capture how empathic people believe they are. Performance tasks can test recognition or inference under controlled conditions. Real-life empathy also depends on motivation, attention, relationship and context.

No single online empathy score can provide a complete assessment of character, diagnosis or relationship safety.

Myths and corrections

What empathy does not mean

Myth

“Empaths absorb everybody's energy.”

Emotional contagion, sensitivity and hypervigilance can feel intense, but “empath” is not a clinical diagnosis or evidence of supernatural perception.

Myth

“High empathy means you are always right about people.”

Empathy remains vulnerable to projection, bias, incomplete information and confident misinterpretation.

Myth

“Empathy requires agreement.”

Understanding another viewpoint does not require accepting its accuracy, morality or consequences.

Myth

“A person either has empathy or does not.”

Empathy has multiple components and varies across people, relationships, emotional states and situations.

Myth

“Feeling overwhelmed proves deep empathy.”

Overwhelm may reflect personal distress, trauma activation or weak emotional boundaries rather than accurate understanding.

Myth

“People with narcissistic traits have no empathy.”

Research indicates variable and compromised empathic functioning, not a universal absence in every person and situation.

Frequently asked questions

Questions about empathy

What is empathy in simple terms?

Empathy is noticing and trying to understand another person's emotional experience, sometimes accompanied by an emotional response of your own.

What are the main types of empathy?

The most common distinction is between cognitive empathy, which involves understanding perspective and emotion, and affective empathy, which involves emotional resonance. Empathic concern and empathic accuracy are also important.

Is empathy the same as sympathy?

No. Sympathy generally means feeling concern or sorrow for somebody. Empathy more specifically involves understanding or resonating with their experience.

Is empathy the same as compassion?

No. Compassion includes concern for suffering and motivation to help. A person may understand another's feelings without being motivated to support them.

Can empathy be learned?

Some components can be strengthened through attentive listening, emotional vocabulary, perspective taking, feedback, reflective practice and greater contact with unfamiliar experiences. Change varies between people and contexts.

Can you have too much empathy?

Intense emotional resonance without regulation can become personal distress. Sustainable empathy includes self and other distinction, compassion and boundaries rather than emotional absorption.

Can empathy be used manipulatively?

Understanding another person's feelings can be used helpfully or strategically. Cognitive perspective taking alone does not guarantee compassion or ethical behaviour.

What is empathic accuracy?

It is the degree to which an interpretation corresponds with what the other person actually reports thinking or feeling. Accuracy requires checking assumptions.

Why do I feel other people's emotions so strongly?

Possible contributors include emotional contagion, high sensitivity, learned monitoring of others, anxiety, trauma-related hypervigilance and difficulty separating personal feelings from another person's signals. Intensity does not always equal accuracy.

Does autism mean a lack of empathy?

No. Autism should not be reduced to an absence of empathy. Communication differences, emotion recognition, cognitive perspective taking, sensory processing and affective concern are distinct, and profiles vary considerably between autistic people.

Do narcissists have empathy?

Empathic functioning in narcissism is complex and variable. Research describes problems in emotional empathy, motivation and responsiveness rather than a simple universal inability to understand all feelings.

Is being an empath a psychological diagnosis?

No. “Empath” is a popular identity term, not a clinical diagnosis. Experiences described by the term may involve sensitivity, emotional contagion, compassion, anxiety or hypervigilance.
