What is gaslighting?

Gaslighting is a pattern of psychological manipulation that undermines another person's confidence in their own perceptions, memories, experiences or understanding of reality.

Gaslighting refers to a pattern in which someone undermines another person's confidence in what they remember, perceive, feel or understand.

It is more specific than lying or disagreeing. The central feature is the destabilisation of another person's trust in their own judgement or sense of reality.

IN DEPTH

Understanding gaslighting in detail

Gaslighting has become one of the most widely used psychological terms on the internet, but its popularity has also caused the term to become blurred. Ordinary disagreement, forgetfulness, lying, selfish behaviour and different interpretations of an event are sometimes all described as gaslighting.

A more useful definition focuses on what happens to the other person's ability to trust their own knowledge. Gaslighting involves behaviour that systematically undermines confidence in memory, perception, judgement or reality.

Researchers have examined gaslighting not only as an individual psychological interaction but also as a relational and social phenomenon involving power, credibility and dependence.

01

Reality is challenged

Events, conversations or experiences may repeatedly be denied, contradicted or reframed.

02

Confidence is weakened

The person may gradually become less certain about their memory, interpretation or judgement.

03

Credibility shifts

The manipulator's account can increasingly become the version that is treated as authoritative.

04

Self trust erodes

The target may begin checking their own recollections, decisions and emotional responses more frequently.

What does gaslighting actually mean?

At its core, gaslighting concerns control over what counts as real, believable or trustworthy within an interaction.

Someone may remember an event clearly, raise it and repeatedly be told that it never happened, that they misunderstood it, that their memory cannot be trusted or that their emotional response proves they are irrational.

When this occurs as part of a sustained pattern, the person's attention may gradually shift away from evaluating the other person's behaviour and towards evaluating themselves.

Gaslighting is not simply lying

A lie attempts to make another person believe something untrue. Gaslighting can go further by attacking the person's confidence in their own ability to determine what is true.

Someone might lie about where they were yesterday. That does not automatically make the behaviour gaslighting. If they repeatedly deny observable events and insist that the other person's memory, perception or judgement is fundamentally unreliable, the interaction begins to resemble the concept more closely.

Disagreement is not automatically gaslighting

Two honest people can remember the same conversation differently. Memory is reconstructive rather than a perfect recording, and genuine disagreement about past events is common.

The presence of contradictory memories alone therefore does not establish psychological manipulation.

Common gaslighting behaviours

Gaslighting does not require a particular script. What matters is the wider pattern and its effect on who is permitted to define reality within the relationship.

  • Repeatedly denying events that the other person remembers.
  • Telling somebody that they are imagining things without a reasonable basis for doing so.
  • Reframing concerns as evidence that the person is irrational, unstable or incapable of remembering accurately.
  • Contradicting previous statements and then denying that those statements were ever made.
  • Using another person's understandable emotional reaction as evidence that their account cannot be trusted.
  • Repeatedly replacing discussion of observable events with attacks on the person's judgement or mental stability.

What can gaslighting sound like?

DENIAL

“That never happened. You invented it.”

MEMORY

“You always remember things incorrectly.”

PERCEPTION

“You are imagining a problem that isn't there.”

EMOTION

“The fact you're upset proves you are irrational.”

These examples require context. A single sentence cannot establish that a relationship contains a pattern of gaslighting.

Why can gaslighting be difficult to recognise?

People normally rely on social feedback to make sense of ambiguous situations. We ask other people what they remember, compare interpretations and sometimes revise our beliefs when somebody gives us new information.

That normal openness to correction can become a vulnerability when a relationship repeatedly supplies misleading feedback about what is real or reasonable.

The problem may be especially difficult when the same person who destabilises someone's confidence is also an important source of reassurance, affection, authority or information.

Gaslighting and power

Sociological research has emphasised that gaslighting can operate within unequal relationships and broader structures of credibility and power. This matters because not every person's account is treated equally in every social environment.

Social status, gender, professional authority, institutional position and existing stereotypes can influence whose interpretation receives greater credibility.

This does not mean that every power imbalance creates gaslighting. It means that the social context can affect whether attempts to undermine another person's reality are successful.

What does research say about gaslighting?

Academic interest in gaslighting has expanded considerably. Paige Sweet's sociological analysis highlighted the role of social inequalities and power in gaslighting within intimate relationships.

Qualitative research by Willis Klein, Shuhong Li and Susan Wood has examined experiences of gaslighting in romantic relationships, including how affectionate and abusive behaviour may coexist over time.

Researchers have also begun developing measurement tools and clearer theoretical frameworks because the popular use of the word has expanded faster than the empirical literature.

EVIDENCE FIRST

Ask what can actually be established

Psychological terminology should help us examine evidence rather than replace it.

  • What specifically was said or done?
  • Is the behaviour isolated or repeated?
  • Are there independent records or witnesses?
  • Is disagreement about an event being confused with manipulation?
  • Is one person repeatedly attacking the other's ability to know what happened?
  • Does the pattern increase dependence on one person's version of reality?

Gaslighting and memory

One reason the subject requires care is that human memory is not perfectly reliable. People can sincerely remember details differently. Memory changes over time and can be affected by attention, expectation, later information and emotion.

Therefore, “you remembered something differently from me” is not enough to demonstrate gaslighting.

The concern becomes more specific when differences in memory are used repeatedly to undermine another person's overall confidence in their ability to perceive and evaluate reality.

Can gaslighting be unintentional?

The question of intention can be difficult. Some definitions emphasise deliberate manipulation, while real interpersonal behaviour can also involve defensiveness, self deception, poor memory and sincerely held but inaccurate beliefs.

For practical purposes it can be useful to distinguish between intention and impact. A person can examine a damaging pattern without pretending to know another person's internal motives.

Gaslighting and narcissism

Gaslighting is frequently described online as a “narcissistic tactic”. That wording is too simplistic.

Gaslighting is not a diagnostic criterion that allows somebody to identify Narcissistic Personality Disorder. People with and without psychiatric diagnoses can behave manipulatively, and observing gaslighting-like behaviour does not establish a personality disorder.

Gaslighting and DARVO

Gaslighting and DARVO can overlap, but they are not interchangeable. Gaslighting focuses on destabilising another person's confidence in reality, perception or memory.

DARVO specifically describes the sequence of denying alleged wrongdoing, attacking the person making the allegation and reversing victim and offender roles.

One interaction could contain both patterns, but neither concept should be used merely as another name for the other.

What can help if you are questioning your reality?

When somebody feels increasingly unable to trust their own judgement, the goal should not be to diagnose the other person. The immediate task is to restore clarity and independent reference points.

  • Write down important events while they are still fresh in memory.
  • Preserve relevant communications where it is lawful and appropriate.
  • Separate observable facts from interpretations.
  • Discuss confusing experiences with trusted independent people.
  • Avoid allowing internet labels to become substitutes for evidence.
  • Seek appropriate professional support when a relationship is affecting safety, wellbeing or mental health.
QUICK KNOWLEDGE

Frequently asked questions

Is every lie gaslighting?

No. Lying and gaslighting can overlap, but gaslighting specifically concerns behaviour that undermines another person's confidence in their own perception, memory or reality.

Is disagreement gaslighting?

No. People regularly disagree or remember events differently. Context and pattern matter.

Is gaslighting a psychiatric diagnosis?

No. Gaslighting describes a pattern of interpersonal or social behaviour. It is not a psychiatric diagnosis.

Can gaslighting happen outside romantic relationships?

Research and academic discussion have considered gaslighting in multiple settings, including workplaces and institutional environments as well as intimate relationships.

Is gaslighting the same as DARVO?

No. They can overlap, but DARVO concerns denial, attack and reversal of victim and offender roles, while gaslighting concerns the undermining of another person's confidence in reality.
