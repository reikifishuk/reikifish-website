Narcissistic Personality Disorder

Narcissistic Personality Disorder, or NPD, is a recognised personality disorder involving persistent difficulties in self functioning, grandiosity, need for admiration, empathy and relationships.

Evidence led · Clinical distinctions · Primary sources included
Prepared by Andy Fish · ReikiFish Knowledge Base · Psychology education
Personality functioning is more complex than a checklist
QUICK ANSWER

Narcissistic Personality Disorder (NPD) is a clinical personality disorder. It involves a pervasive pattern associated with grandiosity, need for admiration, difficulties involving empathy and problems in self and interpersonal functioning.

NPD is not the same as having narcissistic traits. Diagnosis requires substantially more than noticing arrogance, selfishness, entitlement or one unhealthy relationship.

IN DEPTH

Understanding NPD properly

Narcissistic Personality Disorder is one of the most discussed and most misrepresented diagnoses online.

Social media often presents NPD as a synonym for an abusive, manipulative or selfish person. Clinical psychology is considerably more nuanced.

Personality disorders concern enduring patterns in how a person experiences themselves, relates to others, regulates emotions and responds across different situations.

NPD therefore cannot be established from a single behaviour, argument, relationship or online checklist.

Narcissistic traits vs Narcissistic Personality Disorder

Narcissistic traits exist dimensionally in the general population. People can display ambition, self importance, entitlement, admiration seeking or defensiveness without having NPD.

The clinical distinction involves persistence, severity, breadth and impairment.

TRAIT

Narcissistic characteristic

An individual characteristic such as entitlement, grandiosity or admiration seeking.

PATTERN

Elevated narcissistic traits

Multiple characteristics may become more prominent without necessarily constituting a personality disorder.

PATHOLOGY

Pathological narcissism

Significant difficulties regulating self esteem alongside narcissistic grandiosity and vulnerability.

DIAGNOSIS

NPD

A clinical diagnosis requiring an enduring and impairing personality pattern assessed by an appropriately qualified clinician.

The word “narcissist” is not a diagnosis

Calling somebody “a narcissist” after observing harmful behaviour does not establish NPD. It is more accurate to describe the behaviour unless a genuine clinical diagnosis is known.

How is NPD conceptualised clinically?

The DSM-5-TR includes Narcissistic Personality Disorder as a recognised personality disorder.

Its traditional diagnostic description centres on a pervasive pattern involving grandiosity, need for admiration and difficulties with empathy, beginning by early adulthood and occurring across different contexts.

Features considered during clinical assessment can include persistent patterns involving:

  • exaggerated self importance or grandiosity
  • fantasies concerning exceptional success, power, status, beauty or idealised achievement
  • beliefs about being unusually special or exceptional
  • strong needs for admiration
  • entitlement or expectation of special treatment
  • exploitative interpersonal behaviour
  • impaired or limited empathic functioning
  • envy and status comparison
  • arrogant or haughty behaviour and attitudes

This is an educational overview, not a diagnostic instrument. Formal diagnostic criteria must be interpreted by appropriately qualified clinicians within a complete assessment.

The dimensional view of NPD

Modern personality research increasingly looks beyond simple yes-or-no categories.

The DSM alternative model for personality disorders considers impairment in personality functioning alongside pathological personality traits.

01

Identity

How stable and realistic a person's sense of self is and how strongly self definition depends on other people's reactions.

02

Self direction

Goals and personal standards may become strongly connected to approval, exceptionalism, status or entitlement.

03

Empathy

Difficulties recognising, responding to or identifying with other people's feelings and needs can affect interpersonal functioning.

04

Intimacy

Relationships may become disproportionately organised around self esteem regulation, validation or personal gain.

Grandiosity and vulnerability in NPD

One of the most important developments in modern narcissism research is recognition that narcissistic functioning is not always loudly grandiose.

Clinical and empirical research describes both grandiose and vulnerable narcissistic states.

GRANDIOSITY

More externally grandiose states

  • superiority
  • dominance
  • entitlement
  • self enhancement
  • admiration seeking
  • status focus
  • arrogance
VULNERABILITY

More vulnerable states

  • shame
  • insecurity
  • hypersensitivity
  • withdrawal
  • resentment
  • negative emotionality
  • fragile self esteem

These presentations are not necessarily permanently separate. Research and clinical observation indicate that some individuals fluctuate between grandiose and vulnerable states.

NPD and self esteem regulation

NPD is sometimes described as excessive self love, but that description misses an important part of the disorder.

Contemporary models emphasise problems maintaining stable, realistic self esteem.

Approval, admiration, achievement, status and comparison with other people may become important ways of maintaining a desired sense of self.

Experiences such as criticism, rejection, humiliation or failure may therefore carry unusually strong psychological significance.

Does someone with NPD have empathy?

This question requires much more nuance than it usually receives online.

Empathy is multidimensional. It includes processes involved in recognising another person's state, understanding their perspective and emotionally responding to their experience.

Research supports compromised empathic functioning in pathological narcissism and NPD, but does not justify the universal claim that every person with NPD possesses absolutely no empathy.

Empathic responding can vary according to motivation, emotional regulation, self relevance and context.

MYTH CHECK

NPD does not equal “zero empathy”

Empathy difficulties are clinically important, but reducing a complex interpersonal function to either “has empathy” or “has none” loses important psychological information.

NPD in relationships

NPD can create significant relationship difficulties when admiration, entitlement, self esteem regulation or limited mutuality repeatedly interfere with reciprocity.

Difficulties may become particularly visible during criticism, disagreement, perceived rejection or threats to status and self image.

However, no single relationship pattern establishes NPD.

NPD does not automatically mean abuse

This distinction matters ethically and scientifically.

Narcissistic Personality Disorder is a mental health diagnosis. Abuse describes harmful behaviour.

Someone can behave abusively without NPD. Someone can have NPD without every behaviour they display being abusive.

Serious behaviour should be evaluated directly rather than assuming that a psychiatric label is required to explain it.

Does gaslighting prove NPD?

No.

Gaslighting describes behaviour that undermines another person's confidence in their perception, memory or reality. It cannot be used as a stand-alone diagnostic test for NPD.

Does DARVO prove NPD?

No.

DARVO describes denial, attack and reversal of victim and offender roles. It is not a personality disorder diagnosis.

What causes Narcissistic Personality Disorder?

There is no single established cause of NPD.

Personality development reflects complex interactions involving temperament, genetic influences, developmental experiences and wider environmental factors.

Research provides evidence for both heritable and environmental contributions to personality and narcissistic traits.

Claims that NPD always comes from one particular parenting style, childhood trauma or one specific event are therefore too simplistic.

How is NPD assessed?

Appropriate assessment is much more extensive than counting “red flags”.

A clinician may examine:

  • long term personality functioning
  • self image and self esteem regulation
  • interpersonal functioning
  • empathy and intimacy
  • grandiose and vulnerable states
  • patterns across different relationships and environments
  • functional impairment
  • developmental history
  • co-occurring mental health conditions
  • alternative explanations for the presentation

Why differential diagnosis matters

Some features associated with narcissistic functioning can overlap with other clinical presentations.

Mood disturbance, trauma related difficulties, other personality disorders, substance use and situational crises can influence behaviour, self perception and relationships.

This is another reason why remote diagnosis based on social media, relationship stories or checklists is unreliable.

What does current research tell us?

Research during recent decades has considerably expanded the traditional picture of NPD.

Current literature increasingly recognises variability, fluctuations between grandiose and vulnerable states, complex empathic functioning, self esteem regulation and different levels of psychosocial functioning.

Neuroscience research has also investigated narcissistic traits and NPD, but brain findings are not currently a diagnostic test for an individual.

Brain scan claims require caution

Group-level neuroscience findings cannot currently be used to look at an individual brain scan and determine that a person “is a narcissist”.

Can NPD be treated?

The internet frequently claims that NPD cannot be treated and that people with the disorder can never change.

That is too absolute.

Psychotherapy is the principal treatment approach discussed in the clinical literature. Approaches adapted for narcissistic pathology include psychodynamic, transference-focused, mentalisation-informed and cognitive behavioural formulations.

However, the evidence base for NPD-specific treatment remains less developed than for several other disorders, and systematic trials are limited.

Treatment may involve work on self esteem regulation, interpersonal functioning, realistic goals, emotional processing, empathy, reflective capacity and the therapeutic relationship.

Can someone with NPD change?

Improvement is possible, but change may be gradual and individual outcomes vary.

Personality patterns are often longstanding, which means meaningful change usually involves more than simply learning that a diagnosis exists.

Motivation, therapeutic engagement, insight, circumstances and co-occurring difficulties may all influence progress.

Common NPD myths

MYTH Everyone selfish has NPD.

Selfish behaviour alone cannot diagnose a personality disorder.

MYTH NPD means absolutely no empathy.

Empathic functioning is more complex and variable than this claim.

MYTH Everyone with NPD is abusive.

A diagnosis and abusive behaviour are not interchangeable concepts.

MYTH Gaslighting proves NPD.

Gaslighting is a behavioural concept, not a diagnostic test.

MYTH DARVO proves narcissism.

DARVO cannot independently establish a personality disorder.

MYTH People with NPD can never improve.

Clinical literature supports a more nuanced view of treatment and change.

If someone's behaviour is harming you

You do not need to establish a diagnosis before responding to harmful behaviour.

  • Focus on observable behaviour.
  • Consider repeated patterns rather than isolated labels.
  • Maintain appropriate boundaries.
  • Seek independent perspective where needed.
  • Seek qualified mental health support if your wellbeing is affected.
  • Prioritise safety where coercion, threats or violence are involved.

Frequently asked questions about NPD

Is NPD the same as narcissism?

No. Narcissistic traits exist dimensionally. NPD is a clinical personality disorder requiring a much broader pattern and assessment.

Can you have narcissistic traits without NPD?

Yes. This is extremely important. Elevated traits do not automatically constitute a personality disorder.

Is vulnerable narcissism NPD?

Vulnerable narcissism is a research and clinical dimension. It is not itself a separate NPD diagnosis.

Is “covert NPD” a separate diagnosis?

No. “Covert narcissist” is not a separate DSM-5-TR diagnosis, although vulnerable narcissistic presentations are well represented in research.

Can NPD be diagnosed from someone's relationship behaviour?

Not reliably. Diagnosis requires a broader clinical assessment across functioning, contexts and time.

Does a person with NPD know they have it?

Insight varies. It should not be assumed that everybody with NPD either fully recognises or completely lacks awareness of their difficulties.

Can NPD improve with treatment?

Improvement is possible, although treatment can be challenging and the NPD-specific evidence base remains developing.

Is NPD caused by childhood trauma?

There is no single established cause. Development appears to involve multiple biological, psychological and environmental influences.

Does NPD make somebody dangerous?

A diagnosis alone cannot establish whether a particular individual is dangerous. Risk should be assessed from actual behaviour and relevant circumstances.

Can women have NPD?

Yes. NPD is not restricted to men.
