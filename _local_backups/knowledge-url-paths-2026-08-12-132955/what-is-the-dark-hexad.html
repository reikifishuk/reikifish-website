What Is the Dark Hexad?

A critical guide to six proposed dark personality traits: Machiavellianism, narcissism, psychopathy, everyday sadism, spitefulness and greed. This guide separates established research from attractive but misleading internet labels.

Essential context: the Dark Hexad is not a mental-health diagnosis and is not yet a universally standardised scientific model. The Dark Triad and Dark Tetrad have stronger recognition in published research.

Clear definition

What does “Dark Hexad” mean?

The Dark Hexad is a proposed grouping of six socially aversive personality traits. A defensible version expands the Dark Triad—Machiavellianism, narcissism and psychopathy—by considering everyday sadism, spitefulness and greed. These traits may overlap through tendencies such as antagonism, callousness, exploitation, self-interest and accepting harm to others in pursuit of personal benefit.

The term must be used cautiously. “Hexad” simply means a group of six. Unlike a recognised clinical diagnosis, the precise membership of a Dark Hexad has not been settled by one authoritative diagnostic manual, universally accepted measurement instrument or single research tradition. Some writers use “Sinful Six” or “Sinister Six”; other commercial or informal models select different sixth traits.

This page therefore does not pretend that researchers have discovered six fixed personality types. It examines six serious candidates, shows where evidence is stronger or weaker, and explains why traits, motives, cognitive mechanisms and harmful actions must not be treated as interchangeable.

Evidence map

How established is the model?

Confidence should follow the research rather than the drama of the label.

Strongest recognition The Dark Triad has an extensive research literature and established questionnaires, although its three traits still overlap and remain debated.
Growing recognition The Dark Tetrad adds everyday sadism. Evidence supports sadism as relevant, particularly where enjoyment of cruelty distinguishes it from other traits.
Developing proposition Six-trait groupings are useful for exploration, but there is no universally agreed Dark Hexad structure, boundary or universally preferred assessment.
The proposed six

Six dark traits, carefully distinguished

The traits can correlate, but each asks a different psychological question.

1

Machiavellianism

Strategic exploitation

Machiavellianism describes a calculating interpersonal orientation involving cynicism, manipulation, strategic deception and the instrumental use of other people. The person is not necessarily impulsive; patience and planning may be used when these serve a desired outcome.

  • Long-term manipulation and alliance building
  • Selective truth, concealment and impression management
  • A cynical view that other people are primarily self-interested
  • Morality treated as flexible when it obstructs personal goals

Manipulating once does not establish a stable Machiavellian personality trait.

2

Narcissism

Status, entitlement and self-importance

Dark-trait research usually focuses on subclinical narcissistic features such as grandiosity, entitlement, admiration seeking and interpersonal dominance. Narcissism is multidimensional: grandiose and vulnerable presentations should not be collapsed into one stereotype.

  • Elevated self-importance or status preoccupation
  • Entitlement to attention, access or special treatment
  • Sensitivity to threats against status or self-image
  • Relationships used to support identity or admiration

Narcissistic traits are not automatically Narcissistic Personality Disorder.

3

Psychopathy

Callousness, low remorse and disinhibition

In non-clinical dark personality research, psychopathy commonly refers to callousness, shallow affect, low remorse, manipulativeness and varying levels of impulsivity or disinhibition. Clinical and forensic psychopathy is more complex than an online checklist or a brief questionnaire score.

  • Reduced concern about another person’s distress
  • Low guilt or remorse following exploitation
  • Risk taking, stimulation seeking or poor restraint
  • Superficial charm may coexist with emotional detachment

“Psychopath” should not be assigned from conflict, coldness or confidence alone.

4

Everyday sadism

Reward from cruelty or suffering

Everyday sadism concerns deriving pleasure, excitement, dominance or satisfaction from causing, observing or facilitating another person’s discomfort. Its reward component is important: cruelty used merely as a means to another end is not identical to enjoying cruelty itself.

  • Enjoyment of humiliation or deliberate discomfort
  • Provocation continued because the reaction feels rewarding
  • Entertainment from witnessing suffering
  • Cruelty framed as humour, discipline or deserved punishment

A harsh act can be harmful without proving that the person enjoyed suffering.

5

Spitefulness

Accepting personal cost to harm another

Spitefulness is the willingness to damage another person even when doing so also imposes a cost on oneself. This separates spite from straightforward self-interest: retaliation, obstruction or punishment may matter more than one’s own immediate benefit.

  • Sabotaging an outcome rather than allowing another person to benefit
  • Escalating conflict despite predictable personal losses
  • Withholding something valuable primarily to punish
  • Preferring mutual loss over perceived defeat

Anger or one retaliatory decision does not demonstrate dispositional spite.

6

Greed

Insatiable acquisition and never enough

Dispositional greed involves persistent desire for more money, resources, power, status or possessions, together with dissatisfaction at having too little regardless of what is already held. Ambition differs because it need not involve exploitation, insatiability or disregard for others.

  • Acquisition pursued beyond practical need
  • Difficulty experiencing “enough”
  • Competitive accumulation of status, power or resources
  • Other people’s interests discounted when they limit acquisition

Wanting financial security, success or improvement is not inherently greed.

How the idea developed

From three dark traits to six: why the number remains disputed

The Dark Hexad did not appear as a finished clinical theory. It emerged from a much broader attempt to understand socially aversive personality traits: relatively stable tendencies associated with exploitation, callousness, entitlement, cruelty, self-serving conduct or willingness to impose costs on others.

Before 2002

Separate research traditions

Machiavellianism, narcissism and psychopathic features were studied separately. Each had its own history, language and measurement problems. Researchers could observe similarities, but there was no single “dark” grouping that organised all three constructs.

2002

The Dark Triad is named

Delroy Paulhus and Kevin Williams brought Machiavellianism, subclinical narcissism and subclinical psychopathy together as the Dark Triad. Their central point was not that the traits were identical. They described them as overlapping but distinct constructs with shared associations such as disagreeableness, emotional coldness, self-promotion, duplicity and aggression.

This distinction still matters. A strategic manipulator, an admiration-seeking status competitor and a callous impulsive risk taker may cause similar harm for psychologically different reasons.

2009–2013

Everyday sadism becomes a serious fourth candidate

Researchers examined whether enjoyment of cruelty contributed something not adequately represented by the original three traits. Everyday sadism focuses on the rewarding quality of suffering: the person may seek, prolong or enjoy harm rather than merely accepting it as a means to money, status or control.

Behavioural work by Buckels, Jones and Paulhus supported sadism as an independent predictor of behaviour reflecting an appetite for cruelty. This helped establish the Dark Tetrad as a meaningful extension, although measurement and conceptual overlap remain active questions.

2014–2015

Spitefulness and dispositional greed receive dedicated measures

Marcus and colleagues developed a scale for spitefulness: willingness to impose harm on another even when doing so carries a cost to oneself. Seuntjens and colleagues developed a measure of dispositional greed centred on wanting more and remaining dissatisfied with what one already has.

Both constructs appear relevant to dark personality, but relevance does not automatically make them mandatory members of one fixed model containing six traits.

2018 onwards

The Dark Factor asks a different question

Instead of continually adding a fourth, fifth or sixth label, Moshagen, Hilbig and Zettler investigated whether many aversive traits express a more general underlying disposition. Their Dark Factor of Personality, usually shortened to D, concerns pursuing individual benefit while disregarding, accepting or deliberately causing disadvantage to others, accompanied by beliefs that justify the conduct.

This approach does not make individual traits meaningless. It proposes that the traits may be different expressions of a shared dispositional core.

Current position

“Dark Hexad” remains a useful question, not a settled diagnosis

The structure containing six traits used on this page—Machiavellianism, narcissism, psychopathy, everyday sadism, spitefulness and greed—is a transparent, framework informed by evidence. It is not the only conceivable grouping. Researchers have also examined egoism, entitlement, self-interest, moral disengagement and other aversive characteristics.

Why this history matters: internet explanations often present each expansion as if psychology officially discovered another fixed type. The evidence is better understood as an evolving debate about how best to organise overlapping traits.
The membership problem

Which six traits belong in the Dark Hexad?

There is no governing body that has formally selected six members. Any responsible guide must therefore state its inclusion rule rather than quietly presenting a preferred list as universal fact.

Why include spitefulness?

Spite captures costly harm. A purely self-interested actor normally avoids damaging themselves. A spiteful actor may accept lost money, status, opportunity or comfort if the same action punishes the target. That motivation is not fully described by ordinary greed or strategic Machiavellianism.

Why include greed?

Greed captures insatiability: continued acquisition combined with difficulty experiencing enough. It can concern money, possessions, influence, access, attention or status. It overlaps with self-interest but adds persistent wanting and dissatisfaction.

Why not make moral disengagement the sixth trait?

Moral disengagement is often treated as a cognitive process through which people justify conduct. It can help explain how several dark tendencies become action. Calling it merely another trait can obscure its role as a bridge between motive, situation, justification and behaviour.

Why not use entitlement?

Psychological entitlement is highly relevant, particularly to narcissistic and exploitative conduct. However, it already overlaps substantially with important forms of narcissism. A model may study it separately without proving it should replace greed or spite in every grouping of six traits.

What the traits may share

The Dark Factor of Personality

Why do scores on different aversive traits often correlate? One answer is that they partly express a shared tendency. The Dark Factor framework attempts to describe that common ground without pretending that every trait has the same motive or style.

Individual utility

Money, power, dominance, pleasure, status, revenge, control or another personally valued outcome.

D Shared aversive disposition

Disutility for others

Another person’s loss, distress or disadvantage is disregarded, accepted or deliberately produced.

Justifying beliefs are not an optional detail

The shared core is not simply “wanting something”. Most human behaviour pursues some kind of benefit. The darker element is willingness to advance that benefit despite the cost to others, supported by beliefs that make the conduct feel permissible, necessary, deserved or unimportant.

Examples include believing that winners are entitled to exploit losers, that vulnerable people deserve what happens to them, that everybody would behave the same way, that the harm is too small to matter or that one’s own superiority creates an exemption from ordinary rules. These examples describe possible justifications; they do not prove a particular person possesses a general Dark Factor.

Shared core does not mean identical personality

Two people may obtain similar scores on a broad dark factor while differing markedly in expression. One may plan carefully and conceal hostility. Another may react impulsively. A third may seek admiration rather than material benefit. A fourth may find cruelty intrinsically rewarding. General D describes common variance; each specific trait adds its own “flavour”.

Shared tendency Possible expression Why observation alone is insufficient
Self-benefit despite cost to others Exploitation, rule breaking or resource capture The same outcome may arise through pressure, ignorance or competing duties
Low honesty–humility Entitlement, status seeking, insincerity or unfairness One dishonest act cannot establish a stable personality dimension
Antagonism Hostility, combativeness or disregard for cooperation Assertiveness and principled disagreement are not inherently antagonistic traits
Justification of harm Victim blaming, minimisation or displaced responsibility Language requires context and should be compared with conduct and evidence
Overlap without equivalence

How researchers investigate dark traits

Measurement: what a questionnaire can and cannot establish

Dark personality research often relies on questionnaires completed by participants. Participants rate statements about their attitudes, motives or likely behaviour. Researchers then test whether the scores are reliable, relate to other constructs and predict outcomes. This is useful, but it is not equivalent to reading somebody’s character directly.

Dark Triad

Short Dark Triad

A brief measure designed to assess Machiavellianism, narcissism and psychopathy separately. Brevity helps research participation but necessarily reduces nuance.

Dark Triad

Dirty Dozen

A very short twelve-item measure. It is efficient, but critics question whether four items per trait can adequately capture complex constructs.

Sadism

Everyday sadism measures

Instruments examine direct, verbal or vicarious enjoyment of cruelty. Different scales may emphasise different forms and should not be treated as interchangeable.

Spite

Spitefulness Scale

Developed to examine individual differences in accepting personal cost to harm, obstruct or disadvantage somebody else.

Greed

Dispositional Greed Scale

A seven-item measure developed around persistent desire for more and dissatisfaction with what is already possessed.

Shared core

Dark Factor measures

Measures estimate the general common tendency across several aversive traits. Longer and shorter versions trade detail against efficiency.

Six limitations that responsible interpretation must include

  1. Self-presentation: participants may conceal undesirable attitudes, exaggerate toughness, misunderstand questions or answer according to a preferred identity.
  2. Brief scales: reducing a complex construct to a few items increases efficiency but may blur important facets or inflate overlap between traits.
  3. Common-method effects: correlations can partly reflect the fact that the same person answered similarly worded questionnaires at the same time.
  4. Sample limitations: findings from students, online volunteers or one country cannot automatically be generalised to every population.
  5. Group-to-person error: an average association across hundreds of participants does not give certainty about one individual’s behaviour.
  6. Context: opportunity, power, stress, norms, accountability and incentives can influence whether a disposition becomes action.
A high questionnaire score is not a verdict. It does not prove a mental disorder, criminality, future violence, abuse or moral worth. Research assessment, clinical diagnosis, forensic evaluation and everyday observation are different tasks.

From disposition to conduct

Moral disengagement: the bridge, not a seventh trait

Moral disengagement describes cognitive methods that allow someone to perform or tolerate conduct that would ordinarily conflict with their moral standards. Research often treats it as a mediator between dark traits and harmful outcomes, rather than as an agreed member of the Dark Hexad.

Disposition A tendency associated with a trait or motive
Situation Opportunity, threat, reward or social permission
Justification Harm is reframed, minimised or blamed on the target
Behaviour A choice is made within a real context

Common mechanisms include moral justification, sanitising language, advantageous comparison, displacement or diffusion of responsibility, minimising consequences, dehumanisation and blaming the person harmed. This pathway is not deterministic: traits do not force behaviour, and context, values, self-control, accountability and incentives can alter what happens.

Critical distinction

The Dark Hexad is not the same as Cluster B

Dark-trait research Clinical personality disorders
Studies dimensional traits in general or subclinical populations. Diagnosis requires formal criteria, persistence, impairment and qualified assessment.
Narcissism or psychopathy may be measured as research constructs. NPD and ASPD are not established by a Dark Triad questionnaire.
A score indicates relative questionnaire responses within a measurement model. A diagnosis involves history, context, differential assessment and clinical judgement.
The proposed Hexad itself has no official diagnostic status. Clinical terminology should not be used as a weapon during interpersonal conflict.
Myths versus evidence

What the model cannot tell you

Myth: A person either has or does not have a dark trait.

Evidence based view: Traits are generally dimensional and can vary by degree.

Myth: One cruel or selfish act reveals somebody’s personality structure.

Evidence based view: Stable traits require patterns across time and situations.

Myth: High scores prove that somebody is dangerous.

Evidence based view: Group-level associations do not provide certain individual predictions.

Myth: The Dark Hexad is an official psychological diagnosis.

Evidence based view: It is a proposed research grouping with unsettled boundaries.

Myth: Confidence, ambition and strategic thinking are inherently dark.

Evidence based view: Motive, exploitation, disregard for harm and persistent pattern matter.

Myth: Understanding a trait excuses harmful behaviour.

Evidence based view: Explanation and accountability are separate questions.

Frequently asked questions

Dark Hexad questions answered

Is the Dark Hexad scientifically proven?

Research supports studying multiple socially aversive traits, but there is no single universally accepted Dark Hexad model. The Triad and Tetrad currently have clearer research identities.

Can someone be diagnosed with the Dark Hexad?

No. It is not a diagnosis in the DSM or ICD. Only qualified professionals can diagnose recognised mental-health conditions after an appropriate assessment.

Why include greed and spitefulness?

Greed captures insatiable acquisition, while spitefulness captures willingness to accept personal cost to harm another. Neither is fully represented by the classic Triad, although both can overlap with its antagonistic core.

Is moral disengagement part of the Dark Hexad?

Not in the structure containing six traits used here. It is better understood as a cognitive mechanism through which people justify conduct that conflicts with their moral standards.

Does empathy prevent dark behaviour?

Empathy is multidimensional. Understanding another person’s perspective does not guarantee compassionate action, and emotional concern is not the only influence on behaviour. Values, incentives, restraint and accountability matter.

Should I use this model to label an abusive person?

Focus first on observable conduct, its effects, boundaries, evidence and safety. A speculative personality label is not required to recognise manipulation, intimidation, exploitation or harm.
