What is DARVO?

DARVO describes a response pattern in which a person confronted about alleged wrongdoing denies what happened, attacks the person raising the concern and reverses the roles of victim and offender.

A cycle of responsibility reversal
QUICK ANSWER

DARVO stands for Deny, Attack, and Reverse Victim and Offender. The term was introduced by psychologist Jennifer J. Freyd to describe a pattern that can occur when someone is confronted about harmful behaviour.

Importantly, DARVO is a description of a response pattern. It is not a psychiatric diagnosis, and identifying behaviour that resembles DARVO does not by itself establish who is telling the truth.

IN DEPTH

Understanding DARVO in detail

The simplest definition of DARVO is useful, but the concept becomes more important when we look at what happens to a conversation after somebody raises an allegation of harm.

DARVO is not simply disagreement. It describes a particular movement in the interaction. Attention begins with an allegation about one person's behaviour. The allegation is denied. Attention then moves towards the credibility or behaviour of the person who raised it. Finally, the roles can become reversed so that the person originally confronted presents themselves as the victim of the person making the allegation.

This reversal can make an already difficult situation substantially harder to understand because two different questions become mixed together: what actually happened, and who now appears to be behaving badly during the dispute.

01

The original event

Something is alleged to have happened. At this stage the central question should be factual: what occurred, what evidence exists, what is disputed and what can independently be corroborated?

02

The confrontation

The concern is raised. A person may respond in many ways, including agreement, explanation, disagreement, denial, defensiveness or counter allegation.

03

The narrative shift

In a DARVO pattern, attention increasingly shifts away from the original conduct and towards the alleged victim's credibility, motives or behaviour.

04

The role reversal

The person originally confronted may then position themselves as the harmed party while depicting the person who raised the allegation as the real offender.

Why denial alone is not enough

This distinction is essential. People can be falsely accused. An innocent person may strongly deny something they did not do, challenge evidence, defend their reputation or explain why they believe an accusation is mistaken.

For that reason, denial should never be treated as proof of DARVO or proof of guilt. The psychological concept is useful only when considered as a broader pattern and alongside independent evidence.

The attack on credibility

The attack component can be particularly powerful because credibility often matters when outsiders are trying to understand interpersonal disputes. Instead of discussing the original behaviour, attention may become focused on whether the person raising the concern is emotional, angry, inconsistent, unlikeable or supposedly motivated by revenge.

Some of those questions may occasionally be relevant. The danger comes when character arguments replace examination of the original evidence.

How victim and offender roles can become reversed

Role reversal is the element that gives DARVO much of its conceptual importance. The original allegation can become obscured by a second story in which the person confronted describes themselves as the person being persecuted, attacked or mistreated.

Observers may then encounter two apparently competing victim narratives. At that point, confidence, presentation, social support and emotional reactions may influence perception even though none of those things independently establishes what happened.

EVIDENCE FIRST

Separate behaviour from proof

The safest way to use the DARVO concept is as a prompt for better questions, not as a verdict.

  • What was the original allegation?
  • Which parts are agreed and which are disputed?
  • What contemporaneous evidence exists?
  • What can be independently corroborated?
  • Has attention moved away from the original issue?
  • Are character claims being substituted for factual evidence?
  • Are both accounts being evaluated using the same standard?

DARVO and ordinary defensiveness are not identical

Human beings frequently become defensive when criticised. Someone might minimise, explain themselves poorly, become upset or make a counter complaint without consciously attempting to manipulate anybody.

DARVO therefore should not become an internet label for every unpleasant argument. Context, repetition, sequence, evidence and the overall interaction matter.

Can organisations show DARVO-like responses?

DARVO has also been discussed in research concerning institutional responses to allegations and institutional betrayal. At an organisational level, similar dynamics may involve denying that a problem exists, challenging the credibility of people who report it and portraying the institution itself as unfairly attacked.

Again, criticism of an allegation is not automatically evidence of institutional DARVO. The concept is most useful when it encourages careful examination of how responsibility, evidence and credibility are being handled.

Why language matters

Psychological terminology can clarify behaviour, but it can also become another weapon in conflict when labels are used as conclusions. Saying “this person is doing DARVO” can sound definitive even when important facts remain disputed.

More precise language is often stronger: describe the specific denial, the specific attack, the specific role reversal and the evidence relating to each claim. This keeps the focus on observable behaviour rather than amateur diagnosis.

What does DARVO stand for?

01

Deny

The alleged behaviour is rejected, minimised or reframed. The response may focus on why the allegation supposedly cannot be true rather than engaging with the concern itself.

02

Attack

Attention moves towards the credibility, motives, character or behaviour of the person who raised the concern.

03

Reverse

The person originally confronted presents themselves as the injured party while portraying the person raising the concern as the aggressor or offender.

What can DARVO look like?

The important feature is not one particular phrase. It is the movement of the conversation away from the original behaviour and towards an alternative account in which responsibility and victimhood become reversed.

A sequence might begin with a denial, move into criticism of the person raising the issue and end with the person who was confronted claiming that they are actually the one being mistreated.

DENY

“That never happened. You are making it up.”

ATTACK

“You are unstable. Nobody should believe you.”

REVERSE

“I am the person being attacked here. You are abusing me.”

These are illustrative examples, not a diagnostic checklist. A denial on its own is not DARVO, and an innocent person may genuinely deny an accusation.

Why can role reversal be so confusing?

Conflict becomes harder to evaluate when several questions are suddenly competing for attention. Instead of examining the original allegation, people may find themselves debating the accuser's personality, motives, emotional response or past behaviour.

This change of focus matters because observers do not assess interpersonal conflict in a vacuum. Credibility, confidence, social relationships, emotion and the way a story is framed can all affect how competing accounts are perceived.

What does research tell us?

Experimental research has examined how exposure to DARVO-style responses can influence perceptions of victims and alleged perpetrators. Research by Sarah Harsey and colleagues has found that DARVO responses can affect observers' judgements of credibility and responsibility.

More recent experimental work has continued to investigate whether DARVO and related responses can change how third parties evaluate allegations of interpersonal harm.

Important distinction

Research showing that DARVO can influence perception does not mean that every denial, counter allegation or claim of victimisation is manipulative.

Evidence should be evaluated independently. Behavioural labels should never replace careful examination of facts, context and corroborating information.

DARVO is not a mental health diagnosis

DARVO is sometimes discussed online alongside narcissism, psychopathy or personality disorders. That can create a misleading impression that DARVO belongs to one psychiatric condition.

It does not. DARVO describes a possible interpersonal response pattern. Observing it does not establish that somebody has Narcissistic Personality Disorder, Antisocial Personality Disorder or any other mental health condition.

How should you respond if you recognise the pattern?

When a conversation repeatedly moves away from the original issue, it can help to slow the interaction down and separate claims from evidence. The aim is not to “win” a psychological argument. It is to preserve clarity.

  • Return to the original issue rather than chasing every new accusation.
  • Separate what is known from what is assumed.
  • Keep relevant records where doing so is lawful and appropriate.
  • Avoid diagnosing the other person.
  • Use neutral, specific language about observable behaviour.
  • Seek appropriate professional support when safety or wellbeing is affected.

Can DARVO happen in families and relationships?

Patterns involving denial, counterattack and role reversal can arise in many interpersonal settings, including intimate relationships, families, workplaces and institutions.

In highly conflicted situations, competing accusations can become particularly difficult to untangle. This is exactly why the DARVO label should not be used as a shortcut for deciding who is right. Specific claims still require evidence and context.

QUICK KNOWLEDGE

Frequently asked questions

Does DARVO prove somebody is an abuser?

No. DARVO describes a response pattern. It cannot, by itself, determine whether an allegation is true or establish that a particular person is an abuser.

Is denying an allegation automatically DARVO?

No. A person who believes an allegation is false may deny it. DARVO concerns a broader pattern involving denial, attack and reversal of victim and offender roles.

Is DARVO the same as gaslighting?

No. The terms overlap in some discussions but describe different concepts. DARVO specifically refers to denial, attack and reversal of victim and offender roles.

Is DARVO a clinical diagnosis?

No. DARVO is a descriptive psychological concept rather than a diagnosis in psychiatric diagnostic manuals.
