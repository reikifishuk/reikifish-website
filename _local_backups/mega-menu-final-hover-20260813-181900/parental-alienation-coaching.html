CHILD FIRST • FACTS FIRST • STAY STEADY

When adults are in conflict, the child should never become the battleground.

When contact changes and emotions are running high, clarity matters. Slow the situation down, separate what you know from what you fear, and keep the child's wellbeing at the centre of what happens next.

Clarity before reaction
WHEN CONTACT CHANGES

Keep your footing when everything feels uncertain.

Few experiences are more distressing for a parent than feeling their relationship with their child beginning to change, particularly when separation, conflict or difficult adult behaviour surrounds it.

Fear, anger and confusion can create an understandable urge to react quickly or prove what is happening. Yet this is often the moment when calm thinking, measured communication and a clear record of events matter most.

You may not be able to control every decision around you. You can still protect the way you respond, the clarity of what you communicate and the child-focused position you maintain.

Where communication becomes difficult, consistency matters. Staying measured, avoiding retaliatory exchanges and keeping the focus on the child can help create stability when the behaviour of others feels unpredictable.

A STEADIER POSITION

Hold on to what you can control.

When the situation feels chaotic, return to four things that remain yours.

01

Your response

Pause before reacting. Communicate deliberately rather than from the heat of the moment.

02

Your clarity

Separate observable facts from assumptions, interpretations and fears about what may happen.

03

Your child focus

Keep the child's wellbeing ahead of the adult conflict, even when emotions are understandably high.

04

Your next step

Focus on the next useful action rather than trying to solve the entire situation at once.

Calm is not passivity. It gives you room to choose.

HOW COACHING CAN HELP

Stay grounded when the situation is pulling you towards reaction.

Coaching does not decide what happened or determine the truth of allegations. It helps you slow the situation down, separate what you know from what you fear, and respond with greater clarity.

Take the next useful, child-focused step deliberately.

01

Separate fact from interpretation

Distinguish what happened, what was said and what can be evidenced from what you believe may be happening.

02

Regulate communication

Reduce reactive messages and communicate in ways that are calmer, clearer and easier for others to understand.

03

Keep the child out of the middle

Maintain a child-focused position even when the adult relationship has become highly conflicted.

04

Create a factual chronology

Organise important events and communications clearly so patterns can be understood without turning every interaction into accusation.

05

Protect your boundaries

Identify what you can influence, what you cannot control and where firmer limits may help reduce unnecessary conflict.

06

Prepare for difficult conversations

Think through what needs to be communicated before important conversations so that clarity is not lost to emotion.

BEHAVIOUR BEFORE LABELS

Focus on patterns that can actually be described.

Cafcass describes alienating behaviours in terms of ongoing negative attitudes or communication that may undermine a child's relationship with another parent or carer.

01

Look for repeated behaviour rather than trying to interpret one isolated event.

02

Record what happened without adding motives that cannot actually be known.

03

Keep adult allegations away from the child wherever it is safe and possible to do so.

04

Continue asking what the child may be experiencing rather than treating them as evidence for either adult.

WHEN PROFESSIONALS OR COURT ARE INVOLVED

Coaching supports your thinking. It does not replace legal advice.

Whether alienating behaviours, domestic abuse or other concerns are established is a matter for the appropriate professionals and, where proceedings are involved, ultimately the court.

Coaching can help you prepare, communicate clearly, remain regulated and keep your own behaviour aligned with the child-focused position you want to maintain.
