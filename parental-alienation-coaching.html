PARENTAL ALIENATION & ALIENATING BEHAVIOURS

When contact changes, stay calm, factual and child-focused.

Few experiences are more distressing for a parent than feeling a relationship with their child beginning to change, particularly when separation, conflict or difficult adult behaviour surrounds it.

It can create fear, anger, confusion and an overwhelming urge to prove what is happening. Yet this is often exactly when clear thinking, measured communication and emotional steadiness matter most.

Coaching offers somewhere to slow the situation down, separate what you know from what you fear, and focus on the decisions and behaviours that remain within your control.

LOOK BEYOND THE LABEL

A child's reluctance can have more than one explanation.

The term parental alienation is widely used, but current family-justice guidance focuses instead on specific behaviours, their context and their impact on an individual child.

A child may become reluctant, resistant or unwilling to spend time with a parent for different reasons. These can include their own experiences, harmful conflict, fear, domestic abuse, pressure from adults or a combination of circumstances.

That is why it is important not to begin with a diagnosis or assumption. A calmer approach asks what can actually be observed, what remains uncertain and what response best keeps the child at the centre.

HOW COACHING CAN HELP

Stay grounded when the situation is pulling you towards reaction.

Coaching does not decide what happened or determine the truth of allegations. It helps you think more clearly about how you respond to the situation in front of you.

01

Separate fact from interpretation

Distinguish what happened, what was said and what you can evidence from what you believe may be happening.

02

Regulate communication

Reduce reactive messages and communicate in ways that are calmer, clearer and easier for others to understand.

03

Keep the child out of the middle

Maintain a child-focused position even when the adult relationship has become highly conflicted.

04

Create a factual chronology

Organise important events and communications clearly without turning every interaction into an accusation.

05

Protect your boundaries

Identify what you can influence, what you cannot control and where firmer limits may help reduce conflict.

06

Prepare for difficult conversations

Think through what needs to be communicated to solicitors, professionals or other people involved without losing focus.

BEHAVIOUR BEFORE LABELS

Focus on patterns that can actually be described.

Cafcass describes alienating behaviours in terms of ongoing negative attitudes or communication that may undermine a child's relationship with another parent or carer.

01

Look for repeated behaviour rather than trying to interpret one isolated event.

02

Record what happened without adding motives that cannot actually be known.

03

Keep adult allegations away from the child wherever it is safe and possible to do so.

04

Continue asking what the child may be experiencing rather than treating them as evidence for either adult.

WHEN PROFESSIONALS OR COURT ARE INVOLVED

Coaching supports your thinking. It does not replace legal advice.

Whether alienating behaviours, domestic abuse or other concerns are established is a matter for the appropriate professionals and, where proceedings are involved, ultimately the court.

Coaching can help you prepare, communicate clearly, remain regulated and keep your own behaviour aligned with the child-focused position you want to maintain.
